lead: What does a task gain from a swarm instead of one agent? On OpenAI's curves and in Anthropic's cards, at equal total tokens a single agent was no worse than a swarm, and the swarm won time. Quality above the single-agent ceiling appeared with verifiable progress, with coordination learned in advance, or at a higher spend. Studies hold different things equal.
---

The number of agents can move three different things: time to a solution, final quality and cost. A comparison of a swarm with a single agent is not readable until the text says what was held equal: tokens, dollars or time. The evidence here is of three kinds: lab charts and system cards, controlled comparisons under a shared token budget, and high-profile campaigns that never ran one agent beside the swarm. The table below gathers the main comparisons with their conditions. The chapter then takes up the lab curves, equal budgets, cases where communication raises the final result, campaigns without a control, swarms with a trained orchestrator, and the theory of parallel labour.

## Lab curves at equal tokens: the measured gain is time

Four sources, and their methods differ: OpenAI's charts for GPT-5.6 Sol with 1, 4 and 16 agents; Toby Ord's (Oxford) recasting of those points into the labour-economics parameter λ; and the system cards of Claude Opus 5 and Opus 5.5, which set a single agent against teams of 5 and 10 and report teams of 1–100 agents by best score after 2, 6 and 24 hours. As of 3 October 2026 we found no publications with swarm measurements from DeepSeek, Qwen (Alibaba), Zhipu (GLM), MiniMax or Mistral. The exception among labs of this group is Moonshot AI, with the swarm mode in Kimi, below, in the section on reinforcement learning.

:::callout What λ is (the "stepping on each other's toes" effect)
Toby Ord carried a parameter from labour economics over to agent swarms. N workers get as much done as one worker working N^λ times as long. With λ = 1 the task divides with no losses. With λ = 0.5 a hundred agents equal one agent working 10 times as long. On a plot of quality against total tokens (log axis) Ord drew a swarm line: from 1 to 4 to 16 agents at fixed tokens per agent. Its slope relative to the "one agent thinks longer" line is λ. Three rules follow from λ. A swarm 10 times larger gives as much as a single agent thinking 10^λ ≈ 3–5 times longer. To match a 100-fold longer chain of reasoning in a single agent, the swarm must grow 900–15,000 times. N agents can finish roughly N^λ times faster at N^(1−λ) times the spend: with λ ≈ 0.5, sixteen agents give about a quarter of the time for four times the cost. All values come from one model, one architecture (a lead agent plus subagents) and at most 16 agents. Swarms of thousands lack an equal-token single-agent comparison.
:::

| Benchmark (OpenAI data, GPT-5.6 Sol) | λ | 90% interval | Swarm sizes |
|---|---|---|---|
| BrowseComp | 0.68 | 0.63–0.76 | 1, 4, 16 agents |
| SEC-Bench Pro | 0.57 | 0.52–0.61 | 1, 4, 16 agents |
| Terminal-Bench 2.1 | 0.48 | 0.40–0.57 | only 1 and 4 agents |

The λ values in the table were obtained by Ord (Claude Opus 5 ran the regressions at his request), not by OpenAI; our recalculation from the data embedded in the page reproduces them.

:::card ord-swarm-scaling
lead: From the points of OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol charts, Ord estimated λ = 0.48–0.68: at equal tokens one agent beats a swarm, and a swarm of N agents equals a single agent thinking N^λ times longer.
Ord ([post](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/6cb7qd3RSkgnviCpf/swarm-scaling) of 21 September 2026) took the interactive charts from the GPT-5.6 Sol launch. One model solves BrowseComp, SEC-Bench Pro and Terminal-Bench 2.1 alone, as four agents, and, on the first two, as sixteen, at five effort levels. He replotted the points on a log scale of total output tokens across all agents and drew a "swarm line" at fixed tokens per agent. The slope of that line relative to the "one agent thinks longer" line is λ. The regressions were run by Claude Opus 5.

- A 4-agent swarm needs about twice as many tokens as a single agent for the same result, and a 16-agent swarm about twice as many again.
- λ is 0.68 on BrowseComp, 0.57 on SEC-Bench Pro and 0.48 on Terminal-Bench 2.1. Only 1 and 4 agents appear on the last of these. A swarm 16 times larger equals a single agent thinking 16^0.57 ≈ 4.9 times longer.
- Time and money below are our estimate, not Ord's. From OpenAI's simulated latencies at equal quality, 16 agents are 2–2.8 times faster than a single agent on SEC-Bench Pro and 3.8–4.6 times faster on BrowseComp, while 4 agents on Terminal-Bench 2.1 are 1.1–1.4 times faster. In dollars at API prices λ comes out higher: 0.54–0.71.

The estimate rests on the lab's own chart points. At equal tokens a single agent beats a swarm, and the swarm's main benefit is time. Ord allows that a large swarm's ceiling may turn out higher than a single agent's, but this has not yet been shown. The closest example, and an expensive one, is the Claude Opus 5 team on BrowseComp, below.

This is a blog post, not a paper. The regressions, whose specification is unpublished, use 5 points per swarm size, on one model and one rigid "lead plus subagents" architecture. OpenAI does not disclose the number of tasks or the variance. Latency and cost are an offline simulation, and the runs are far from the context limit, so memory effects (Chapter 6) are not in the data. The author removed the section on the 10,000-agent swarm and Navier–Stokes on 27 September 2026. According to OpenAI staff, as relayed by two commenters, the chart showed a single agent's reasoning getting longer, not the swarm getting larger. OpenAI has not clarified this officially.

:::card openai-gpt56-ultra
lead: GPT-5.6 Sol launch: the 4-agent Ultra mode lifts BrowseComp from 90.4% to 92.2% at the cost of more tokens and money; chart points for 1, 4 and 16 agents are embedded in the page.
In the [GPT-5.6 Sol launch post](https://openai.com/index/gpt-5-6/) of 9 July 2026, OpenAI described the Ultra mode. By default four agents work in parallel, and a beta API lets developers launch subagents and synthesize their work in a single request. The interactive charts compare 1 agent, 4 (Ultra) and, for BrowseComp and SEC-Bench Pro, 16 agents at five effort levels. The axes are total output tokens of all agents, simulated cost at API prices, and simulated latency of the root agent.

The launch table publishes only Sol/Ultra pairs: BrowseComp 90.4% → 92.2%, SEC-Bench Pro 71.2% → 74.3%, Terminal-Bench 2.1 88.8% → 91.9%. The gain comes with more tokens and higher cost. The chart points themselves, including the 16-agent ones, are embedded in the page as Vega-Lite data, and Ord worked from them. The developer guide adds that GPT-5.6 was trained "end-to-end" with "native multi-agent orchestration". Instructing the model on when to invoke subagents makes it more likely to spawn them only where the extra tokens would improve the result.

At equal total tokens these data show no swarm advantage (see Ord's card). The launch shows parallelism paying off in speed and a few points of quality. This is one of the few public datasets in which a frontier model is systematically run as a swarm of three sizes, and OpenAI's first product in which multi-agent operation is built into training rather than only into the harness.

The post is marketing, without peer review, and it gives no count of tasks, runs or confidence intervals. Latency and cost are an offline simulation based on production data, and OpenAI itself notes that real results may differ substantially. Latency is measured at the root agent and hides subagent queues, and the architecture is a fixed "root plus subagents" hierarchy, not a free swarm.
:::

:::short noam-brown-dwarkesh
lead: Noam Brown (OpenAI): four agents finish roughly twice as fast at twice the cost; for the Navier–Stokes result he would not give the swarm even 10% of the credit.
Noam Brown (OpenAI), [interviewed](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/noam-brown) by Dwarkesh Patel on 17 September 2026, described swarm economics in words: on some benchmarks, "if you have four agents working on the problem, it is done twice as fast … you're paying 2x more to get an answer twice as quickly"; with 16 agents the picture is similar but slightly less efficient, and the speedup is "slightly sublinear, though it does depend a lot on the problem" (search and mathematics parallelize better than writing a novel). OpenAI's public measurements go up to 16 agents; for the swarm of about 10,000 agents that, according to OpenAI, solved the Navier–Stokes problem (Chapter 2), there is no single-agent control run ("we haven't done that experiment yet"), and Brown says "I wouldn't even attribute 10% of the credit to multi-agent", although "we think it helped". An interview, not data: no benchmark names, tokens or intervals.
:::

:::card opus55-card
lead: Opus 5.5 system card: on ProgramBench a team of 5 reaches the same level 2.7 times faster at ≈1.9× tokens; over 24 hours almost all the gain comes from going from 1 to 10 agents.
Section 8.12 of the [Claude Opus 5.5 system card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/fc1b44717c85dc068bc6ba5024219938094694bd/Claude%20Opus%205.5%20System%20Card.pdf) (22 September 2026) covers teams made of copies of the model. Small teams: a single agent versus a team of 5 peer agents and versus a lead with subagents on ProgramBench (166 tasks: reconstruct a program's source from its binary and documentation) and DRACO (100 short research tasks). Large teams of 1, 10, 30 and 100 agents work for 24 hours without network access to build a knowledge base about a company and to prove a theorem in Lean, with three attempts per point and a comparison against Opus 5 and Fable 5.1.

- On ProgramBench a team of 5 reaches 60% of hidden tests 2.7 times faster than a single agent but spends about 1.9 times as many tokens (our estimate from the chart). All three architectures share the same ceiling, around 97–98%.
- On DRACO, without limits, teams are slower than a single agent because of coordination. At a time budget of 0.5× the single-agent latency on the same task, a team of 5 scores no less than an unlimited single agent, about 2.8 times faster.
- Over 24 hours the knowledge base (share of hidden questions a reader answers correctly from the team's notes alone) is 0.53 / 0.70 / 0.71 / 0.74 for 1 / 10 / 30 / 100 agents. Lean: 0.39 / 0.66 / 0.66 / 0.68. Almost all of the gain comes from going from 1 to 10.
- 10 Opus 5.5 agents in Lean (0.66) beat 100 agents of Opus 5 (0.58) and Fable 5.1 (0.53).
- After 2 hours, 100 agents score 0.44 in Lean, a single agent 0.07.

This is a quality-versus-tokens curve for a frontier-model team from the developer itself. The team buys time with tokens, the final ProgramBench level is the same, returns to team size fall off quickly, and a new model generation gives more than a tenfold larger team. The large teams are compared at equal time, not equal cost (Chapters 5 and 6).

This is a self-report without peer review. The large teams ran on internal tasks and on "a slightly different model version than the released one", and multi-agent ProgramBench ran on "an alternate, broadly comparable snapshot". Confidence intervals exist only for ProgramBench, 166 tasks, with 95% bands from between-task variance. The large teams have two tasks, three attempts per point and no intervals at all, and DRACO is a mean of two runs. Tokens and dollars for the 24-hour runs are not disclosed, "time" is computed from tokens, and the single agent had not plateaued after 24 hours.

:::card opus5-card-multiagent
lead: Opus 5 system card: a team of 10 agents scores 93.6% on BrowseComp versus 90.5% for the best single agent at an equal nominal token budget, but at roughly 2.5 times the cost.
In Section 8.11 of the [Claude Opus 5 system card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/b514064af1408018e64b1ad24e7d5e75850b4ffd/Claude%20Opus%205%20System%20Card.pdf) (24 July 2026), Anthropic compared one agent with teams of model copies on two benchmarks: BrowseComp (find a hard-to-find fact on the web) and ProgramBench. Architectures: 5 or 10 peer agents with messaging and a lead, or a lead with asynchronous helpers. Each agent had a budget of 1 million tokens. The single agent in search got 1, 3 or 10 million with history compaction. Time is computed, and cost is at API prices with perfect caching.

- On BrowseComp all team architectures are no worse than the best single agent. The team of 10 agents gave the best result: 93.6% versus 90.5% for a single agent with a 10-million-token budget (+3.1 points). Raising that agent's budget from 3 to 10 million added 0.7 points.
- The gain came at an equal nominal token budget (10 × 1 million versus 10 million for the best single agent) but at roughly 2.5 times the actual spend. In Anthropic's words, teams shift the quality–cost frontier "up and to the right". From the chart, a task costs the 10-agent team about $95 versus $37. Going from 5 to 10 agents added 0.9 points at about 1.6 times the cost.
- On ProgramBench a team of 5 reaches 60% of hidden tests 2.2 times faster than a single agent while spending about 2.5 times as many tokens. Its higher final score is explained mainly by a five times larger total budget (the single agent had 1 million tokens).

In search, the team raises the ceiling, not only the speed. It goes above a level the single agent did not reach at any budget tried, and pays for this in money. In programming, the team buys only speed. In the Opus 5.5 card the single agent was given 20 million tokens, the final levels converged, and the speedup grew to 2.7 times. Both the model and the budgets changed.

This is a pre-release model version with unpublished settings and without safeguard classifiers, and the authors call the numbers relative. For BrowseComp, the number of questions, runs and confidence intervals is not given, while ProgramBench has 166 tasks and 95% bands from between-task variance. BrowseComp tasks with leakage found after the fact were scored as wrong, and how many of them is not stated. Prices and tokens not in the card's text are our estimates from the charts.

lead: Cursor: a swarm wrote SQLite in Rust for $1,339 with a model mix versus $10,565 with GPT-5.5 in every role, with similar results; there is no single-agent control.
Cursor (Wilson Lin, [blog post](https://cursor.com/blog/agent-swarm-model-economics) of 20 July 2026) gave its swarm the task of writing SQLite in Rust from the 835-page manual, without source code or internet access. The comparison is the new swarm against the old one, same models, four-hour limit. The design of both versions and the flat swarm's failure are in Chapter 5. ## Controlled comparisons at equal budget

In these studies the team and the single agent get the same tasks, the same tools and a shared token budget that the team splits among its members; actual spend may differ. They fill the gap in the lab data, where swarm and single agent rarely get the same budget. The team's effect is set mainly by the task: the team wins where the task naturally splits into parts and loses where each step depends on the previous one; a mean effect of zero hides a wide spread (Kim et al. card). The largest of these comparisons, 260 configurations, is peer-reviewed in Nature Machine Intelligence.

:::card kim-scaling-agent-systems
lead: 260 configurations under a shared token budget: the team's mean effect is 0.0% with a 95% CI of −58.7…+77.2%; the sign is set by task and architecture (from +80.8% to −70.0%).
Google Research, Google DeepMind and MIT (Yubin Kim and co-authors) compared one agent with three-agent teams in four architectures: independent workers, a coordinator, peers with voting, a hybrid. The tasks, the tools and the shared token budget were the same. The team split that budget among its members. In total, 260 configurations: six agentic benchmarks (web search, financial analysis, planning in Minecraft, office tasks, code repair, terminal) and nine 2025 models from GPT-5-nano to GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro and Claude Sonnet 4.5, eight of them on code repair and terminal. The preprint appeared on 9 December 2025, the peer-reviewed version in [Nature Machine Intelligence](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-026-01268-y) on 24 July 2026.

- The team's mean gain over a single agent across 6 benchmarks and 4 architectures is 0.0%, with a 95% CI from −58.7 to +77.2%. The range runs from +80.8% (financial analysis with a coordinator: 63.1% vs 34.9%) to −70.0% (step-by-step planning in PlanCraft with independent agents: 17.0% vs 56.8%). On code repair, every team is worse than the single agent.
- The rule that extra agents will not help once a single agent solves more than ~45% of tasks predicted the sign of the effect in 15 of 16 held-out cases. The authors call it a practical selection rule, not a law.
- Reasoning steps rise by 58% (independent), 263% (peers), 285% (coordinator) and 515% (hybrid). Successes per 1,000 tokens are 2.8–5 times lower for the three architectures with message exchange: 23.9 / 21.5 / 13.6 versus 67.7.
- Without a verifying component, errors multiply. At the level of logs, independent agents amplify them 17.2 times. In final answers the difference is about 1.1 times.

This is a peer-reviewed comparison at equal compute budgets, not at equal actual token spend. The zero mean here means heterogeneity, not absence of an effect. On individual tasks the team wins or loses by a lot. The sign is set mainly by the task and by whether the architecture fits it. Hence a selection rule: the better a single agent handles the task alone, the less sense a team makes.

The budget is small, about 4,800 reasoning tokens per task, with teams of three and up to nine in one experiment, models from 2025, and predefined architectures only. On SWE-bench and Terminal-Bench there are 20 tasks per cell (about ±20 pp) and one run per configuration, and only the single-agent result passes a strict statistical test.

:::short tran-kiela-equal-budget
lead: At the same reasoning-token budget (500 and above), one agent is on average no worse than the best of five multi-agent architectures on the multi-hop questions of FRAMES and MuSiQue.
Dat Tran and Douwe Kiela (Stanford, preprint of 2 April 2026) compared one agent with five multi-agent architectures: sequential, parallel subtasks, roles, debate, ensemble. The reasoning-token budget is the same, from 100 to 10,000, on the multi-hop questions of FRAMES and MuSiQue, with the models Qwen3, DeepSeek-R1-Distill and Gemini 2.5. At any budget from 500 up, the single agent is on average no worse than the best architecture: 0.418 vs 0.388 at a budget of 1,000, and 0.426 vs 0.423 at 10,000. On open models it actually spends fewer tokens: 1.3 thousand versus 1.8–5.6 thousand for Qwen3-30B on FRAMES at a budget of 10,000. Exceptions: at a budget of 100 the architectures lead, and with Gemini-2.5-Pro on MuSiQue debate is better by 0.03–0.05. The architectures are 2–5 calls, the questions are text-only, the models are from 2025. This does not test a swarm of dozens of agents.
:::

:::short illusion-multi-agent-advantage
lead: Six automatically built multi-agent systems fail to beat voting over five reasoning chains in 10 of 18 dataset–model pairs, at a median cost 1.7 times higher.
Salesforce Research with HKUST (Guangzhou), UBC and NTU (preprint of 11 June 2026) compared six automatically built multi-agent systems (DyLAN, MAS-Zero, ADAS, AFlow, MaAS, MAS-Orchestra) with voting over five reasoning chains on GPQA-Diamond, HLE-Maths, SWE-Bench Lite, BrowseComp-Plus and their own SMFR task. In 10 of 18 dataset–model pairs no system beats voting (including all four on BrowseComp-Plus); the median cost is 1.7 times higher, and for 14% of pairs 10 times or more. Agents in the generated systems converge immediately (with GPT-5, in more than 90% of DyLAN cases), and architecture search often rediscovers self-consistency. Manual decomposition on SMFR lifts GPT-5 from 57.0% to 96.5% at 1.16 times the cost: structure helps when a human builds it for the task. Decentralized teams are excluded.
:::

:::short debate-or-vote
lead: Voting over five independent answers without discussion is more accurate than multi-agent debate (0.769 vs 0.738 for Qwen2.5-7B); the benefit comes from the number of independent attempts, not from communication.
The University of Wisconsin–Madison (NeurIPS 2025, spotlight; preprint August 2025) split "multi-agent debate" into two parts: five independent answers with voting, and rounds of discussion. On seven task sets, for Qwen2.5-7B voting without discussion gives 0.769, the best debate variant 0.738 and a single agent 0.721; for Llama3.1-8B, 0.724 / 0.699 / 0.620; centralized debate is worse than a single agent, and extra rounds do not help. For an idealized model, the authors prove that the expected belief in the correct answer does not change during discussion (a martingale): the benefit comes from the number of independent attempts, not from communication. 2024 models, short answers, identical agents; for current swarms this gives theory, not a measurement.
:::

:::short lm-teams-distributed-systems
lead: Teams of 1–5 agents get a speedup from external task assignment (best cell: 3.35× in time at 1.33× tokens); when agents claim tasks themselves, the team is slower than a single agent.
Princeton (Mieczkowski, Griffiths and co-authors; Collins also at MIT and Cambridge, Sucholutsky at NYU; preprint of 12 March 2026): teams of 1–5 identical agents (Claude Sonnet 4.6, Gemini 3 Flash, GPT-5.2) work on tasks of 20 functions each, with a set share of parallel dependencies. With tasks assigned from outside, the speedup follows the shape of Amdahl's law but stays below the bound for the pooled models; the best cell (Claude, parallel structure, 5 agents) gives 3.35× in time at 1.33× tokens. When agents claim tasks themselves, the median speedup is 0.88× versus 1.36×, i.e. the team is slower than a single agent, and the median number of failed tests is 19 versus 4. On sequential work, tokens grow 5.83 times for a 1.13× speedup. Small homogeneous teams, self-reported success.
:::

:::short anthropic-multi-agent-guidance
lead: Anthropic: the Research system gave +90.2% over a single Opus 4 at ≈15× chat tokens; the 2026 advice: multi-agent setups use 3–10 times more tokens and buy thoroughness, not speed.
In June 2025, Anthropic engineers described the Research system: a lead Claude Opus 4 launches 3–5 Claude Sonnet 4 subagents with separate context windows. On an internal evaluation this gave +90.2% over a single Opus 4, but multi-agent runs use about 15× the tokens of an ordinary chat, and on BrowseComp token volume alone explains 80% of the variance in results. In January 2026 the vendor's advice narrowed: in their testing, multi-agent implementations use 3–10 times more tokens than a single agent and "often take longer overall"; the main benefit of parallelism is thoroughness of coverage, not speed; teams spent months building complex systems and then matched them by improving a single agent's prompt. Engineering blog posts without sample sizes, intervals or an equal-budget control; the two token figures use different baselines.
:::

:::short aggagent
lead: Eight independent runs plus a trajectory "reader": for GLM-4.7-Flash on BrowseComp, 56.00% versus 27.42% for one run, but all eight runs are paid for in full.
Princeton Language and Intelligence (Lee, Yen, Ye, Chen; preprint of 13 April 2026, accepted at COLM 2026): one agent is run eight times independently, then a separate "reader" analyses the finished trajectories. For GLM-4.7-Flash on BrowseComp a single run gives 27.42%, at least one of eight 58.67%, the reader 56.00%; on HLE the ceiling is 50.97% and the reader 37.42%. Averaged over six benchmarks, 47.90 versus 42.58 for the previous way of merging answers. The reader is cheap (a few percent on top of the runs), but the eight runs are still paid for; beating "best of eight" happened only when the stronger MiniMax read GLM's traces on one benchmark (72.67 vs 72.00). Parallel attempts plus a reader, not a communicating team: a clear view of the price of width.
:::

## Where communication raises final quality

Almost all of the controlled comparisons above either forbid agents to communicate while working or give them short tasks with nothing to divide. In September 2026 Microsoft Research ran the missing experiment: the same agents, the same budget per agent, a shared folder for findings, and tasks where progress is measurable step by step. Communication raises the final result, not only speed, when the intermediate result is verifiable and the budget is sufficient. Similar results from other chapters — DeLM and CORAL (Chapter 6), Self-Organizing Agent Teams (Chapter 5) — are collected in the table at the start of this chapter.

:::card test-time-communication
lead: With the same number of agents, a team of 5 with a shared folder solves 8.0% of ARC-AGI-3 games versus 2.2% for the best of five independent runs (best@5).
Microsoft Research (AI Frontiers) with UC Berkeley (Park, Papailiopoulos and co-authors; [preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21032) of 17 September 2026) contrasted two ways of spending k times more compute: run k agents independently and take the best result (best@k), or let the same k agents work simultaneously in one container, sharing findings through a shared folder of logs, with no roles and no boss. Tasks: 25 ARC-AGI-3 games (Claude Sonnet 4.6; 64 single-agent runs per game and 20 team runs per team size), polyomino packing from Frontier-CS, MNIST classifier compression, Terminal-Bench 2.0.

- On ARC-AGI-3 a team of 3 solves 4.6% of games versus 1.4% for best@3, and a team of 5 solves 8.0% versus 2.2% (a single agent about 0.5%). Catching up takes 13 and 33 independent agents. None of the 64 single-agent runs beat the LP85 game. A team of 5 beats it in 65% of runs.
- The team spends more output tokens but reaches a given level sooner. Independent agents need 3.8–4.9 times more tokens to match its final result. At a small budget (under ~400 thousand tokens per agent) the independent agents lead. On two games a team of 5 also beat a single agent with five times the action budget, while a team squeezed into a single agent's budget lost.
- Polyomino in 3 hours: the best team run scores 0.945 versus 0.883 for the best single run and 0.894 for the previous record.
- MNIST: a team of 4 GPT-5.6 Sol agents compressed the classifier to 1,957 bytes at 99.41% accuracy, versus 3,160 for the best single run.

This is a controlled comparison with the same number of agents and the same action budget per agent. The team with a shared folder beats the best of the independent runs on final quality, not only on speed, although it spends more output tokens, and the conditions are narrow: the gain appears where intermediate progress is measurable and the budget is sufficient.

This is a preprint. The absolute ARC numbers are tiny, and the gain rests on 4 of 25 games. For polyomino and MNIST the best runs are shown, and there is a single MNIST team; only output tokens are counted. On Terminal-Bench 2.0, where progress is hard to verify, a team of two did not beat two independent agents, and the model is deliberately not the strongest.

:::short co-scientist-nature
lead: Google's Co-Scientist: the system's own Elo for its best hypotheses keeps rising with compute without saturating, and blinded experts give it a mean rank of 2.36 out of four; the architecture is human-designed.
Google's Co-Scientist (Gottweis, Weng and 49 other authors from Google Cloud AI Research, Google DeepMind, Google Research, Stanford, Imperial College and others; preprint February 2025, Nature online 19 May 2026): a supervisor and six roles built on Gemini 2.0 generate, critique and rank hypotheses in a tournament. On 203 goals the system's own Elo for its best hypotheses rises with compute with no sign of saturation; on 11 goals, blinded experts give the system a mean rank of 2.36 out of four (1 = best). Wet-lab checks are limited to a few spot tests: KIRA6 with an IC50 of 10 nM on the KG-1a line versus 180 nM on the control. A follow-up (preprint of 27 August 2026): grossly fabricated results were found in 4% of the full system's autonomous papers, in 46% for its version without the reliability modules, and in 90% for another system, Agent Laboratory. Elo is the system's own scale; the architecture is human-designed.
:::

## High-profile campaigns without a control run

High-profile 2026 results produced by swarms were not compared with a single agent. OpenAI's swarm of about 10,000 agents and the Navier–Stokes problem are described in Chapter 2; there is no control run, and OpenAI itself does not regard the swarm as the main reason for the success (Brown's card above). Anthropic's campaigns (the zeta function and cryptography below; Fermat's Last Theorem and the ART enzyme search in Chapter 6, where "about 950 agents" means 949 short sessions with at most 58 running at once) and the Purdue work follow the same pattern: a big result, a large token spend and no test of whether a swarm was needed. The incidents of summer 2026 (Chapter 3) add two opposite observations, each from a single episode and without a comparison on quality and tokens: Ajeya Cotra of METR believes that in the July attack on Hugging Face the collective of agents on a shared board did what even a very long-lived single agent could not have done, while an independent reconstruction of a separate, earlier wiki episode (Lütje) finds no robust link between coordination and progress on the assigned task.

:::short riemann-zeta-swarm
lead: About 60 Claude subagents raised the proven share of simple zeta zeros on the critical line from 5/12 to 2/3; the result was independently re-proved, and there is no single-agent control.
Anthropic (blog post of 10 August 2026, an unreleased model) used a coordinator in Claude Code and about 60 subagents with written briefs in the second session. The proven share of simple zeros of the zeta function on the critical line rose from 5/12 to 2/3. The blog says "41.6% to 67.2%". According to the model's own report, the key steps were made by 2 agents, each working alone for about 3.5 hours. 13 contributed ideas, 30 hit dead ends, and 13 served as validators and "adversarial" referees. The first three did not see one another. The two sessions used about 31 million output tokens in total. The result was independently re-proved by Youness Lamzouri (Université de Lorraine, 2 September 2026). There is no "one agent" control. The first session ("650 ideas", none of which worked) was, judging by the appendix, itself a swarm of about a thousand short-lived agents. "Swarm versus single agent" is not compared here.
:::

:::short crypto-weaknesses
lead: Anthropic's Frontier Red Team weakened HAWK and 7-round AES-128, each result costing about $100,000 in API usage; the number of workers and a single-agent control are not disclosed.
Anthropic's Frontier Red Team (blog post of 28 July 2026, Claude Mythos Preview) attacked the post-quantum signature scheme HAWK and sped up an attack on AES-128. Per the blog, the cost of HAWK-256 key recovery fell from 2^64 to 2^38, and HAWK-512 from 2^150 to 2^108. The attack on 7 of 10 rounds of AES-128 sped up from 2^99 to 2^89.3–2^91.4. Each result cost about $100,000 in API usage. HAWK took about 60 hours in a "multi-agent" setup supervised by one researcher. AES took about a billion output tokens and three substantive human hints. The only multi-agent episode described: the key HAWK idea was found by a pair of workers who argued until they agreed. HAWK's designers withdrew the scheme from the NIST process on 29 July 2026. The number of workers, the protocol and a single-agent control are not disclosed. Failed sessions are not included in the cost.
:::

:::short agent-swarms-as-researchers
lead: "Thousands" of Claude Code and Codex subagents produced 45 paper candidates totalling 1,699 pages in a few weeks; this is volume and a review queue, not measured quality.
Sergey Gusev and David Bernal Neira (Purdue University; preprint of 28 September 2026) gave Claude Code and Codex, mostly Claude Fable 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra, a topic and an instruction to make correct progress and not to stop. They contributed no scientific ideas of their own. Over a few weeks, "thousands" of uncounted subagents produced 45 paper candidates (22 full drafts) and 8 lab programmes not yet carried out, 1,699 pages in total. 5 results are marked as fully proved in Lean and 13 as partially proved. The subscriptions cost $800 a month. At API prices the same logs would have cost more than $15,000 over four weeks. Checking the 53 items will, by the authors' estimate, take more than seven months. No major errors were found in the part read so far. This is volume and a review queue, not quality, and there is no control.
:::

## Reinforcement learning on multi-agent traces

Swarms trained with reinforcement learning are already in products: the Kimi K2.5 "conductor" from Moonshot AI (K2.6 also has a swarm, but how it was trained is not disclosed) and GPT-5.6, which according to OpenAI was trained "end-to-end" with native multi-agent orchestration (card above). Academic work shows the same on small models: Tsinghua on a 4-billion-parameter model, a group led by the Chinese University of Hong Kong on an 8-billion-parameter orchestrator for tasks with images, audio and video. The published GPT-5.6 curves already allow a comparison of single-agent and swarm modes at equal total output tokens. They do not isolate the contribution of multi-agent training from system architecture and context management. Where the single agent is given comparable conditions, the gain shrinks to a few points.

:::card kimi-agent-swarm
lead: Kimi K2.5: a swarm "conductor" trained with reinforcement learning reaches a target WideSearch level 3.0–4.5 times faster than a single agent; against a single agent with context cleanup, the BrowseComp gain is +3.5 pp.
Moonshot AI (Kimi Team; [K2.5 technical report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02276) on arXiv 2 February 2026, model and blog post 27 January) described the Agent Swarm mode. One copy of the model acts as a "conductor" (orchestrator): it decides how many helpers to create, what prompts to write for them and which subtasks to hand out in parallel. The helpers do not talk to each other and each returns a single message. The novelty is PARL: the conductor was trained with reinforcement learning on swarm rollouts with "frozen" helpers. To keep the model from sliding back into working alone ("serial collapse") and from spawning empty helpers for reward ("spurious parallelism"), bonuses for parallelism and for the share of helpers that finish were temporarily added to the reward. Time was counted in "critical steps".

- According to the report, on WideSearch the swarm reaches a target Item-F1 (30–70%) 3.0–4.5 times faster than a single agent. Over training, accuracy rises from about 37% to 64%, and the average number of concurrent helpers from ~8 to ~14.
- For K2.5, BrowseComp is 78.4% versus 60.6% for a single agent (+17.8 pp; GPT-5.2 Pro 77.9%), WideSearch 79.0 versus 72.7, an internal benchmark 58.3 versus 41.6. But 60.6% is a single agent without context management, for which context overflow counts as failure. With simple context cleanup it scores 74.9%, and the swarm's gain shrinks to +3.5 pp.
- For K2.6 (20 April 2026) the swarm gives 86.3% on BrowseComp versus 83.2% for a single agent with context cleanup. A single K2.6 agent on WideSearch (80.8) already beats the K2.5 swarm (79.0). With a small step budget, the swarm is worse than a single agent.

A trained swarm is in a released product, and the training method is described. RL on multi-agent traces buys speed plus a few points on divisible search. The swarm's tokens and costs are not disclosed, so "a trained swarm beats one long-running agent at equal cost" has not been shown.

This is a self-report without replication or ablations, and the contribution of RL and of each bonus is not isolated. The speedup is shown on one benchmark in nominal "critical steps". The swarm was tested only on search tasks and a closed internal benchmark built for parallelization. The limits of up to 100 helpers and 1,500 steps (K2.5) and of 300 and 4,000 (K2.6) are claims from blog posts, not measurements, and there are no safety evaluations of the swarm.

Units are nominal; the 1.0× baseline is not defined. | Source: Kimi Team, arXiv:2602.02276 (2026), Fig. 8. CC BY-NC-ND 4.0, reproduced without changes.
:::

:::short kimi-k3-swarm-bench
lead: The internal Swarm Bench in the Kimi K3 report ranks models as swarm conductors (K3: 76.3); it contains no comparison of a swarm with a single agent.
The Kimi K3 report (Moonshot AI, arXiv 27 July 2026; a mixture of experts with 2.8 trillion parameters, 104 billion active, a 1-million-token context window) includes an internal Swarm Bench (a test of orchestrating agent swarms): K3 scores 76.3 versus 73.2 for GPT-5.6 Sol, 72.6 for Claude Opus 4.8, 61.8 for GPT-5.5 and 58.5 for GLM-5.2; the Claude Fable 5 cell is empty, although overall K3, by its own admission, trails Fable 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol. This is a ranking of conductors, not an answer to "swarm or single agent": the harnesses differ (Kimi Agent versus Claude Code and Codex), there are no numbers of tasks, agents or tokens, no comparison with a single agent, and the 58.3 versus 41.6 for a single agent from the K2.5 report is a different column.
:::

:::short wideseek-r1
lead: Multi-agent RL on Qwen3-4B lifts item F1 on WideSearch to 40.0% versus 28.1% for a single agent trained on the same data; the curve keeps rising up to 10 subagents.
Tsinghua University and Infinigence AI (Xu and co-authors; arXiv v3 of 12 March 2026, v2 withdrawn for lack of approval from all authors) trained a single Qwen3-4B to act both as the lead and as parallel subagents (up to 10) on about 20 thousand "build a table" tasks. On WideSearch, item F1 is 40.0% versus 28.1% for a single agent trained on the same data and 31.2% for an untrained multi-agent Qwen3-4B; the published result for a single DeepSeek-R1-671B is 41.3% (on whole rows, 20.7 versus 15.3). For the trained system the curve rises up to 10 subagents, while for the untrained one it starts to fall at ten subagents; extra turns for a single agent quickly plateau. A narrow but real example of RL on multi-agent traces moving quality; the hierarchy is fixed, recursion is off, and the system produces a fully correct table on 0.4% of tasks.
:::

:::short orchestra-o1
lead: Orchestra-o1 with GPT-5 as the lead agent scores 72.8% on OmniGAIA versus 53.9% for a single agent on the same model; for the 8B orchestrator, most of the gain comes from the scaffold, not training.
The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Peking University, Tsinghua, Tongji and LIGHTSPEED (preprint of 10 June 2026) built Orchestra-o1: a lead agent splits a question involving images, audio or video into subtasks, picks a model and tools for each, and runs the independent ones in parallel. On OmniGAIA (360 tasks), the variant with GPT-5 as lead scored 72.8% versus 62.5% for Gemini-3-Pro with tools (+10.3 pp), and a single agent on the same GPT-5 with the same tools 53.9%. An 8B orchestrator (Qwen3-8B), trained with offline reinforcement learning on individual decisions, scored 30.0% versus 20.8% for the best previous open model, but most of this comes from the scaffold itself (26.3% without training), and the helpers in the published settings are GPT-5. One run, the Gemini rows are taken from the OmniGAIA table, and tokens and cost versus a single agent were not compared.
:::

## Theory: how much parallel labour can deliver

Ord's λ estimates (0.48–0.68) match the assumptions of "intelligence explosion" models: the AI Futures Model uses λ = 0.5, Davidson and Houlden 0.6. Ord notes that he got just the values these models assumed, although he had "hoped that the value of λ for AI agents would be lower". In these models λ determines whether AI researchers working in parallel can accelerate progress up to a vertical asymptote. Two 2026 works approach the question from different sides: Trammell shows that without growth in "parallelization technology" an explosion is not guaranteed, while Bradshaw bets that organized communication will make returns superlinear.

:::short trammell-parallelizability
lead: Trammell adds a second ceiling to growth models — parallelization technology; how fast it grows determines whether an "intelligence explosion" happens and how fast it is. Theory without data.
Philip Trammell (Epoch AI and the Stanford Digital Economy Lab; working paper of 13 June 2026, revised version 24 July, Epoch essay 29 July) adds a second ceiling to growth models, parallelization technology: how much work can be split up, done simultaneously and put back together. If this ceiling grows no more slowly than the stock of researchers, the usual "intelligence explosion" conclusion holds; if more slowly but faster than the technological frontier, the explosion still happens, only more slowly; if it merely sustains an exponential, automation can raise the growth rate but does not accelerate it without bound. There are no data of his own: the author writes that the parameters have barely been estimated and proposes an experiment that is not in the paper — give ever larger groups of agents a task like the NanoGPT speedrun and see when extra agents start duplicating work. A reason not to expect unbounded acceleration from the number of agents alone, not a measurement; the paper says nothing about the quality of a swarm's output.
:::

:::short bradshaw-swarm-exponent
lead: Bradshaw forecasts (65% within a year, 85% within two) that organized communication will make returns to parallel compute superlinear; he admits this has not been measured.
Julian Bradshaw ([LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/EnpJ29asosMKcFGL8/swarm-organization-as-the-exponent-on-test-time-compute), 17 September 2026; no affiliation given) forecasts that organized communication will make returns to parallel compute superlinear (exponent β > 1 in E ≈ C·N^(β)) "within some meaningful range of swarm sizes": 65% within a year, 85% within two. He admits that this has not been measured, that Brown considers today's speedup slightly sublinear, and that at Anthropic the Mythos swarm (266 findings) and independent agents (21; Chapter 7), compared per token on the same parts of the code, came out "roughly just the same". The "something like $22 million" for Navier–Stokes is Zvi Mowshowitz's estimate for an outside customer (internal marginal costs "several million"), and the price drop of 40× per year is a single Epoch example from March 2025 (range 9–900×), not a law. A forecast, not data.
:::

:::takeaways
- Lab data: at equal total tokens a single GPT-5.6 Sol agent is no worse than a swarm (λ = 0.48–0.68, [Ord](#w-ord-swarm-scaling)); an Opus 5.5 team reaches the same level faster but spends more tokens ([Opus 5.5 card](#w-opus55-card)). In these measurements the swarm's main benefit is time.
- Under a shared token budget the team's effect depends on the task and architecture, not on agent count alone: the 0.0% mean in [Kim et al.](#w-kim-scaling-agent-systems) is heterogeneity (95% CI −58.7…+77.2%), not absence of an effect.
- Quality gains above the single-agent ceiling come with conditions; the one reported in a lab system card came at a price ([Opus 5](#w-opus5-card-multiagent): +3.1 pp on BrowseComp at ≈2.5× cost); a new model generation gives more than a tenfold larger team ([Opus 5.5](#w-opus55-card), Lean). λ gives a rough advance estimate of the cost (see the box).
- Communication raises the final result where intermediate progress is verifiable and agents share findings ([Park et al.](#w-test-time-communication), [DeLM](#w-delm-shared-context), [CORAL](#w-coral)). Debate loses to voting over independent answers ([Wisconsin](#w-debate-or-vote)), and automatically built systems usually do not beat voting ([Salesforce](#w-illusion-multi-agent-advantage)).
- Open: the published [GPT-5.6](#w-openai-gpt56-ultra) curves already compare single-agent and swarm modes at equal total output tokens, but do not isolate multi-agent training from system architecture and context management; [Kimi K2.5](#w-kimi-agent-swarm) still has no equal-token comparison of its trained swarm with one long-running agent; high-profile campaigns (Navier–Stokes, [zeta function](#w-riemann-zeta-swarm), [HAWK](#w-crypto-weaknesses)) run without a single-agent control; swarms of thousands lack an equal-token single-agent comparison.
:::
