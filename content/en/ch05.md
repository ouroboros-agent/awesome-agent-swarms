Give identical agents a shared channel, a mailbox, a board or git, and no assigned roles. Can they split the work, and is the organization that appears better than a scheme drawn in advance, or better than one agent at an equal budget? Equal-budget comparisons here mean matched tokens or compute, not matched wall-clock time. A structure showing up in the logs, and that structure beating an alternative, are different results. The chapter keeps them apart: first what equal agents actually do when the logs are open, then what companies found after a flat swarm failed on their own code, then controlled comparisons, then rules an outer loop selects before the task starts. The summary table records what each study equalized. Physical swarms at the end are a contrast in method. One word, "self-organization", covers three mechanisms in these papers, and they do not support the same conclusion.

:::callout Three different kinds of "self-organization"
The works in this chapter use one word for three different things, and the conclusions differ.

1. **Emergence on the fly.** Equal agents with a shared channel divide the work themselves, during the task: Agensh, Carlini's compiler, AutoScientists, AI Village (Chapter 10). This is self-organization in the pure sense.
2. **Learned organization.** An outer loop works out the rules of collaboration before the work starts, and they are then frozen: Self-Organizing Agent Teams (a debrief after each task), Sakana's Conductor and TRINITY (reinforcement learning and evolutionary search). No human draws the scheme, but equal agents do not invent it from scratch either: at Sakana a trained dispatcher picks the workers and roles for each task, and in Self-Organizing Agent Teams the frozen strategies set the order of the conversation, while the division of work on a new task forms and changes as the work proceeds.
3. **Designed through iteration.** Engineers read failure logs and redraw the scheme: Cursor, Anthropic Labs, Cognition. The authors sometimes call the result "emergent", but that is their interpretation, not an experiment.
:::

## Equal agents with a shared channel: what the logs show

This section collects runs in which many identical agents get a shared channel, a task and time, while humans stay only in the outer loop. Two such runs with open data appeared in 2026: a thousand Microsoft Research agents on one program and sixteen Anthropic agents on a compiler; a third observation, without open logs, comes from the Claude Opus 5.5 system card. All three show that structure emerges; none compares it with a designed one.

:::card agensh-1024
lead: Up to 1,024 identical agents with no dispatcher hold together and put forward roles on their own; the pandoc pass rate rises from 33.89% (one agent) to 55.06%. Time (6 h) was equalized, not compute.
Zhihao Zhan, Ting Song, Li Dong and colleagues at Microsoft Research, in "Agensh: Scaling Organizational Intelligence to 1,024 Agents", handed one large programming task to an organization of identical agents and no dispatcher. Every worker is the same model, GPT-5.6-sol, in the Copilot harness, with the same prompt except for the worker ID. They share a Gitea Git server, a Mattermost chat and a board for claims, findings and failures. The task is from ProgramBench: in 6 hours, with no Internet, rebuild an existing program (FFmpeg, gromacs, pandoc, PHP-src or ctags) given only the ability to run it. Teams of 1, 8, 32 and 128 agents ran on all five tasks, and 1,024 agents on pandoc only. The prompt still prescribes a detailed work cycle, so "no structure" means "no boss".

The mean share of hidden tests passed rises with team size, but not evenly.

- Mean over the tasks: 19.31% with one agent, 20.68% with 8, 26.52% with 32 and 28.78% with 128 (+9.47 pp).
- Pandoc: 33.89 → 41.75 → 43.11 → 50.94 → 55.06% for 1, 8, 32, 128 and 1,024 agents, about +2 pp per doubling (our estimate). On pandoc, 128 agents exceeded 30% by minute 30; the single agent did not get there in two hours.
- The curves jump. On ctags, 8 agents do worse than one (18.47% vs 25.82%). On PHP-src, 128 agents fell from 28.47% in the second hour to 12.35% at the finish, and the paper does not explain the drop.

Pandoc and ctags supply almost all of the gain.

Cooperation in the logs grows with team size: agreements on interfaces at 8 agents, then reviewers chosen by experience, a negotiated integration protocol, and self-nominated "integrators" at 128 and 1,024. A replay of the 1,024-agent run (our count) shows 11,111 pull requests, 2,800 merged (25%), and 148,079 board entries, 56,472 of them FAIL. Identical prompts and a shared channel of messages, board and git are enough for roles to appear among a thousand participants. Nothing in the study compares that with a designed hierarchy. The authors' claim that an orchestrator would be the bottleneck is an argument, not a measurement. Time was matched and compute was not: tokens were not counted, the 128- and 1,024-agent teams received roughly 117 and 800 times the agent-hours of one agent (our estimate), and the single agent was still improving at hour six. It stayed on the task for all 6 hours only because of a "stop hook". No repeats are reported. This is a preprint, and the code is unavailable (404).

:::card anthropic-c-compiler
lead: In two weeks, 16 Claude Opus 4.6 copies with no boss or messaging wrote a C compiler that builds a bootable Linux kernel; parallelism broke where the task was indivisible. One run, no control.
Nicholas Carlini's "Building a C compiler with a team of parallel Claudes" is a two-week run of 16 copies of Claude Opus 4.6. Each copy sat in its own container with a clone of one shared git repository and the job of writing, in Rust, a C compiler that builds the Linux kernel. There was no boss, and the agents did not message each other. An agent claimed a task with a lock file in git; a double claim made the push fail, and the second agent had to pick something else. Notes and a queue of ideas also lived in files. Every session started with an empty context.

By Carlini's account the run used nearly 2,000 sessions, 2 billion input tokens and 140 million output tokens, for just under $20,000. The compiler he describes as "100,000-line" builds a bootable Linux 6.9 kernel for x86, ARM and RISC-V, compiles QEMU, SQLite, PostgreSQL and Doom, and passes 99% of the tests in most suites. The repository history, counted here, shows 3,982 commits from 23 January to 5 February 2026, 2,005 claims, 14 team restarts and 186.7 thousand lines of Rust. While the kernel build was one indivisible task, all 16 agents hit the same bug, wrote the same fix and overwrote each other. The split that unblocked them was a human scheme: a reference GCC compiles some files and the new compiler the rest, so the work becomes independent pieces.

The open git history is what makes the run readable. Agents choose tasks and carry a real project to a result, and the same history shows where self-organization stops. A human assigned the five specialist roles (deduplication, speed, efficiency of the generated code, architecture critic, documentation), designed the tests, the CI and the reference scheme, and restarted the team about 14 times. There is no single-agent control at the same budget and no other team size. The prompt, the tests and the harness are unpublished. The compiler is also weaker than the headline. Carlini says GCC still compiles the 16-bit x86 boot code, the compiler's own assembler and linker are rough, and the code it generates is slower than GCC with optimizations disabled. Late changes broke earlier ones, which he treats as a limit of the model. John Regehr found 34 miscompilation bugs. SPEC CPU2017 programs showed on average more than 70% lower performance than when built with GCC, and some of the bugs match bugs in the hobby compiler chibicc.

The review's chart from repository data, not an Anthropic illustration. | Source: our analysis of the anthropics/claudes-c-compiler repository history (data CC0-1.0).
:::

The third observation is in the Claude Opus 5.5 system card (Anthropic, 22 September 2026; the economics of these teams are in Chapter 4). A hundred agents build a hierarchy themselves, and the task sets its shape. Teams of 1, 10, 30 and 100 agents worked for 24 hours on one machine on two tasks: a knowledge base about a company, and a theorem in Lean. The harness designated only the lead. Who messaged whom, and who handed work to whom, the agents decided. On the Lean task the 100-agent team formed two tiers. The lead stayed in contact with 12 "sub-leads". Each sub-lead handed work to its own group of 3–9 helpers and reported back, and five more helpers reported to the lead directly. "Sub-lead" is the authors' post-hoc definition: a helper from whom at least three other helpers received more messages than from any other agent. On the knowledge base the same hundred stayed flat. The lead split the records in advance, and the helpers barely talked. The structure was read from the transcripts of one 100-agent team per task. The card does not measure any gain of the two-tier scheme over the flat one, and team size, as Chapter 4 shows, adds almost nothing to the result beyond 10 agents.

:::short autoscientists
lead: A team with no central planner beats a single-agent loop, but at 4.4× the money.
Shanghua Gao, Ada Fang and Marinka Zitnik (Harvard), in "AutoScientists: Self-Organizing Agent Teams for Long-Running Scientific Experimentation", run long scientific searches on Claude Sonnet 4.6. The default team is 6 experimenters and 3 analysts. Teams discuss directions, vote, and reorganize when a direction stalls. On 24 BioML-Bench tasks the mean leaderboard percentile is 74.40 against 66.07 for the single-agent Autoresearch loop. Starting from an already strong champion on training a small GPT, the team accepted 7 improvements out of 93 attempts and the single agent 0 out of 100. Self-organization is limited: roles are set at launch, and after a vote one agent, the alphabetically last analyst, records who is on which team. Tokens cost about 4.4 times as much ($3,985 vs $910). A 14-agent team is worse than a smaller one on every task of the size check. The report is a preprint.
:::

:::short mollick-dot-and-swarm
lead: A popular-essay version of the idea that agents no longer need to be assigned roles in advance; in his Navier–Stokes example, according to OpenAI's page, people steered the swarm's work.
Ethan Mollick's essay "The Dot and the Swarm" (1 October 2026) argues that a swarm divides the work by itself. His example is the Navier–Stokes effort (the chronicle is in Chapter 2, the economics in Chapter 4). OpenAI's page says the result came from a group of "on the order of 10,000 concurrent agents" in about 88 hours, and that the whole effort took 2.7 million messages and about 130 billion output tokens. Mollick writes "thousands" of agents. The same OpenAI page describes the human role: people chose the problem, moved agents onto it from other problems, prompted them with the Euler result and cross-pollinated groups through Codex. That is thinner than an org chart and thicker than "no one organized them". The Clay prize has not been awarded. This is a blog, not a measurement.
:::

## Engineering experience: flat swarm vs hierarchy

Companies that run swarms on real codebases reached similar conclusions independently: a swarm of equals did not work for them, and a scheme drawn by people did. Cursor described the path in three posts. It started with a swarm of equals and redrew the scheme from failure logs.

:::card cursor-scaling
lead: The flat "swarm of equals" failed: 20 agents worked at the throughput of one to three. A hierarchy of planners and workers, found by engineers from the logs, worked. Company posts, no control.
Wilson Lin's three Cursor posts (14 January, 5 February and 20 July 2026), "Scaling long-running autonomous coding", "Towards self-driving codebases" and "Agent swarms and the new model economics", describe hundreds of coding agents spending weeks on one project without humans: a browser in Rust from scratch, then SQLite in Rust from the documentation alone. The first version was a swarm of equals, same status and a shared coordination file with locks, then optimistic concurrency control and a "wait for another agent" tool.

The flat scheme failed quickly. Twenty agents worked at the throughput of two or three (first post) or one to three (second). Agents held locks too long, forgot to release them, or used them incorrectly. Dropping locks cut the overhead, and without a hierarchy the agents became risk-averse, made small safe changes, and nobody took the hard end-to-end tasks. What worked, across four design generations read off the logs, was a root planner, recursive sub-planners with narrow areas, and workers on their own copies of the repository. Each worker does one task without coordinating and hands in a written report. The central integrator was removed as a bottleneck.

The browser run peaked at about 1,000 commits per hour and went a week without intervention. The public repository has 29,858 commits and 1.74 million lines of Rust in the main src folder. On SQLite in 4 hours, the new swarm passed 73–85% of hidden tests against 11–77% for the old one. With Grok 4.5 the old swarm ran away: more than 70,000 merge conflicts, one file edited by 1,173 agents, stopped before the two-hour mark. The new one had fewer than 1,000 conflicts. The flat swarm was tried on a real project with three coordination mechanisms, and it did not work. People designed the hierarchy that did. "Emergent" in the second post is the author's reading of that redesign, not an experiment that let the organization appear. Chapter 4 covers what the runs cost. These are company posts about Cursor's own system: no paper, no harness code, no logs, no comparison with one agent at the same budget, and no count of attempts per configuration. Lin asks readers to look at trends. The flat scheme was tried only briefly, on the models of winter 2025/26, and with three mechanisms. The browser did not build at publication; a fixed version appeared 2–3 days later.
!fig cursor-scaling_fig1.png | The failed flat scheme: four equal agents and one shared coordination file through which they all try to coordinate. | Source: © Cursor, Wilson Lin, "Towards self-driving codebases", cursor.com/blog (2026), diagram 1; quoted for review.
!fig cursor-scaling_fig2.png | The final scheme: the planner delegates to sub-planners (recursively) and to workers; all workers push to a shared Git. | Source: © Cursor, Wilson Lin, "Towards self-driving codebases", cursor.com/blog (2026), diagram 4; quoted for review.
:::

:::short cognition-multi-agents
lead: Cognition's experience: parallel writing by several agents does not work; a narrow class of schemes does, in which agents contribute intelligence but one agent writes. Vendor opinion without tables.
Walden Yan (Cognition, makers of Devin) argued in "Don't Build Multi-Agents" (June 2025) against multi-agent systems: parallel writers make implicit decisions that conflict, and one linear agent with the full context is more reliable. In "Multi-Agents: What's Actually Working" (April 2026) he says that still holds for parallel writing, and he names the schemes that do work. The Devin Review reviewer, starting from a clean context, finds on average 2 bugs per PR, about 58% of them severe, and pairing Claude and GPT helps on the hardest cases. He calls unstructured swarms mostly a distraction, names "map-reduce-and-manage" as the practical shape, and notes that messaging between agents does not emerge by itself, because the models were not trained for it. The numbers come with no sample size and no baseline.
:::

:::short harness-design-long-running
lead: On one prompt, a three-agent harness with assigned roles built a playable game maker where solo Opus 4.5 did not, but took 6 hours and $200 vs 20 minutes and $9, from an expanded spec.
Prithvi Rajasekaran (Anthropic Labs, 24 March 2026), in "Harness design for long-running application development", built three agents with assigned roles: a planner, a generator, and an evaluator that clicks through the app with Playwright. The only direct comparison he describes in detail is one prompt for a retro game maker. Solo Claude Opus 4.5 worked 20 minutes for $9. The harness worked 6 hours for $200. In the solo version the entities did not respond to input. In the harness version one could control a character and play, although Rajasekaran got stuck at one wall. The task was not the same: the planner expanded the one-sentence prompt into 16 features. His lesson is about the harness, not about the score. Every component is an assumption about what the model cannot yet do alone, and it comes out as soon as a newer model manages without it. With Opus 4.6 the sprints disappeared. The planner and the evaluator stayed, evaluation moved to the end of the run, and, he says, the evaluator pays off on tasks beyond what the model does reliably solo. One run, no benchmark.
:::

The [Anthropic campaign](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-discovers-novel-enzyme-system) in which agents searched protein databases for new enzyme systems (23 September 2026; Chapter 6 goes through the memory) is organized from the top as well. The announcement's "roughly 950 agents" are 949 Claude Mythos 5 sessions over 21.5 hours, with at most 58 able to run at once. Worker, supervisor, curator of the shared knowledge base, and editor were roles set in advance. The five stages ran as a chain: the next opened only when a script accepted the previous one. Initiative also came from below. The agents opened 98 of the 119 tasks themselves. Neither a flat team nor a single agent was tried on the same task, so the campaign is a working practice, not a comparison.

## Controlled comparisons: equal agents vs a coordinator, a designed scheme and a single agent

The company stories have no control arm. The controlled studies that do, mostly from 2026, usually find the same thing when the alternative is a coordinator, a prescribed scheme, or one agent doing the same work: the peer team loses or ties. The exceptions later in the section are specific. An external turn order with self-chosen roles can beat a central coordinator. Five peers on ProgramBench can beat a lead-and-subagents scheme on speed and tokens, with the same final quality. Those are not a general win for an unstructured swarm.

:::card teams-hold-experts-back
lead: With no expert named, a team without roles does not beat its best member on any of five benchmarks; on ranking tasks non-experts average the expert's view with theirs, and the gap grows with size.
Aneesh Pappu, Batu El and colleagues, in "Multi-Agent Teams Hold Experts Back" (ICML 2026), tested a pure swarm of equals. Several different models, with no roles and no rules, discuss a task for four rounds and give a joint answer. The teams were built on purpose from one strong model and several weak ones, for example GPT-5 with GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Haiku. The suite is five AI benchmarks and three psychology ranking tasks in which one or several members hold the knowledge.

When the expert is not named, the team does not beat its best model on any benchmark. It does beat the average member and simple voting.

- Team vs best model: 86.0% vs 86.5% (MMLU-Pro), 76 vs 78 (GPQA), 51 vs 52 (SimpleQA), 28 vs 29 (HLE), 63 vs 73.5 (MATH-500).
- If the team could pick, on every question, the answer of whoever was right, it would score 91.8 / 88.8 / 62.3 / 47.5 / 79.0%. The shortfall from that ceiling is 6.3% to 41.1% relative.
- Naming the expert, a hint that does not exist in practice, lets the team beat the best model on four benchmarks of five, and it still misses the ceiling.

On the psychology tasks the team's error is 17–113% higher than the expert's. Members do not defer even to a named expert.

- Non-experts treat the expert's view as something to average with their own, an "integrative compromise" (r = 0.55 and 0.69 with the loss, significant on two tasks of three), and experts give in.
- Larger teams (2 → 4 → 8) have a larger gap (r = 0.32–0.59). Four experts together do about as well as one, so the cause is unequal knowledge, not conversational noise.
- One saboteur barely hurts (26.0 vs 26.7).

The hope under test is that equal agents will organize themselves around the strongest member. They average that member out, which is why the authors recommend explicit roles. The next paper by the same first and senior authors, below, is about learned organization rather than this free discussion. The mechanism was measured on cheap 2024 models (Claude 3.5 Haiku, GPT-4o-mini). GPT-5, Opus 4.5 and Sonnet 4.5 appear only in the benchmark part, 100 questions each and no confidence intervals, so gaps of 0.5–2 pp may be chance. The format is one question, four rounds, no tools and no memory. Long-running swarms were not tested. The team makes 25–28 model calls per task instead of one (our estimate from the protocol), and tokens are not reported. The explanation through training for agreeableness is the authors' hypothesis.

Schematic, no data. | Source: Pappu et al., ICML 2026 / arXiv:2602.01011, Fig. 1. arXiv non-exclusive license; quoted for review.

:::

:::short cooperbench
lead: The "curse of coordination": a pair of agents loses, on average across five models, 41% of single-agent capability.
Arpandeep Khatua, Hao Zhu and colleagues at Stanford and SAP Labs, in "CooperBench: Why Coding Agents Cannot be Your Teammates Yet", built 652 tasks. Two agents receive different features that overlap in the code. Their changes are merged, and the tests for both features run. GPT-5 succeeds as a pair 0.28 of the time against 0.48 when one agent gets both features. Claude Sonnet 4.5 scores 0.26 vs 0.47, MiniMax-M2 0.14 vs 0.36. Chat reduces merge conflicts and does not raise success. On 46 tasks, growing the team from 2 to 3 and 4 agents drops success from 68.6% to 46.5% and 30.0%. Role division appears on its own, rarely. Of 50 analysed failures, 42% are a wrong picture of the partner, 32% broken commitments and 26% failed exchanges. The study is a preprint about pairs: small teams rather than large swarms.
:::

:::short worse-together
lead: A team with one agent per user is worse than a single coordinator in every environment, and a mailbox does not close the gap.
Sahan Paliskara, Nattaput Namchittai and Andrew Lampinen, in "Worse Together: How Performance Breaks Down in Multi-User Multi-Agent Teams" (preprint, 30 September 2026), describe 77 scenarios in four environments. Each user has an agent, and the resource is shared: an API budget, a merge queue, a clinic, a personal assistant placing a joint order. On the API budget, peer-to-peer reaches 30% of the optimum with Claude Opus 5 and 12% with Sonnet 5, against 64% and 32% for a coordinator. Without a communication channel those peer shares are 7% and 2%. As the team grows from 4 to 16 agents, the share of Sonnet 5 agents that act falls from 66% to 10%. In the clinic a silent Opus 5 team beats a communicating one (81.0% vs 69.5%). The gap closes with an appointed lead, a strict instruction, or a block on the order until the other agents' messages have been read. That is an assigned role, not a hierarchy the agents built. The benchmark has not been released yet.
:::

:::short when-agents-coordinate
lead: The task, not the agents, sets the shape of the communication network, and the word "coordinator" in the prompt changes nothing.
Giuseppe Destefanis and Tomaso Aste, in "When Agents Coordinate: Measuring Coordination in Multi-Agent AI Coding", recorded 1,902 runs of teams of 1 to 16 Claude Sonnet 4.6 agents in Claude Code on two synthetic Python tasks, plus 244 runs in a sealed environment. The agents do build a network. A shared specification produces a dense network (mean degree 5.47 at 8 agents), a pipeline a sparse one (2.99), and at 16 agents direct links nearly vanish (0.28). The phrase "you are the coordinator" creates neither a hub nor a gain. In the sealed replication a flat team and a team with a "coordinator" are not significantly different (20/30 vs 21/32, p = 1.00). An eight-step chain passes in 9 of 10 runs with two and four agents and in 0 of 10 with eight, once a rounding rule falls on the boundary between two owners. One model, and large variance between sessions.
:::

:::short orch-embodied
lead: A hierarchy drawn by a human beats both a scheme drawn by a model and dynamic leadership.
Zhengran Ji, Jonathan Hyun and Boyuan Chen at Duke, in "Organizational Principles Enable Collective Intelligence in Embodied AI", ran teams of up to 50 heterogeneous agents in the CREW-Wildfire firefighting simulator: 25 missions, eight models, five seeds. The ORCH scheme is drawn before the start and is not changed during the mission. The abstract reports +63.97% in score and +74.29% in efficiency for the human scheme relative to four earlier multi-agent algorithms, and +43.63% and +52.53% for the model-drawn scheme. Raw means are not in the text. Episode success is 53.1% for the human scheme, 42.2% for the model with a critic, 35.2% without a critic, and 6.9–13.5% for the baseline systems, including CAMON with its "emerging leadership" (1,000 runs each). It is a simulation, with no single-agent comparison, and a language model did the error analysis.
:::

:::short dochkina-drop-hierarchy
lead: An external turn order with self-selected roles beats both a central coordinator and fully simultaneous work.
Victoria Dochkina's "Drop the Hierarchy and Roles: How Self-Organizing LLM Agents Outperform Designed Structures" compares coordination protocols for groups of 4 to 256 agents on synthetic tasks. Another model judges quality. The winning protocol, Sequential, keeps an external turn order. Agents choose only their role. On GPT-4.1-mini with 8 agents the scores are 0.724 for Sequential, 0.640 for a central coordinator and 0.503 for fully simultaneous Shared (+44%, Cohen's d 1.86). On Claude Sonnet 4.6, DeepSeek v3.2 and GLM-5, with 16 agents and GPT-5.4 as judge, Sequential beats the coordinator by 14.1%, 12.4% and 12.2%. Shared was not re-run. From 64 to 256 agents quality does not change (about 0.96). Agents invent role names almost every time (5,006 unique names), and at 256 agents about 45% withdraw on their own. One author, and the data are not released. The 25,000 runs stated in the abstract do not match the table (about 20,810).
:::

A counterexample in favour of equals is in the Claude Opus 5.5 system card, §8.12.1–8.12.2, with more of the economics in Chapter 4. Five peer agents, each holding the full task, the same tools and a messaging channel, were compared with a scheme in which a lead spawns subagents and those subagents see only the lead's instructions. On ProgramBench, measured on "an alternate, broadly comparable snapshot" of the model, the peer five reached 60% of hidden tests faster, about 38 vs 66 minutes of derived latency, and spent fewer tokens to get there, about 1.1 vs 2 million. Both pairs of numbers are our estimates from the charts. The final ceiling is the same for both schemes and for the single agent. On DRACO, under a tight time budget, the lead spawns subagents much less often and prefers to work alone, while a pre-assembled team keeps dividing the work. The gain is speed and tokens, not final quality. People designed both schemes. In this comparison the flat team of equals did not lose to the hierarchy.

## Learned organization: an outer loop selects the rules

If equal agents do not find a good structure during the task, and a scheme drawn by people goes stale, a third option is to let a separate loop select the rules in advance, by reinforcement learning or by a debrief after each task. This is not a swarm of equals. No human draws the scheme, and the agents do not invent it as they work. Only the division of labour adapts to the specific task. That is the second kind in the box at the start of the chapter.

:::card sat-teams
lead: The learned organization of three different models beats both a single model and hand-designed protocols (66.7% vs 58.7% for one model at roughly the same budget), but on some benchmarks the gain almost vanishes.
Aneesh Pappu and James Zou, the same first and senior authors as "Multi-Agent Teams Hold Experts Back", with second author Mirac Suzgun and colleagues at Stanford, Together AI and Emory, tried to teach a team from its own experience. The paper is "Self-Organizing Agent Teams Learn to Reason Together". Three different models, o3-mini, Claude Sonnet 4 and DeepSeek-V3, solve 15 AIME 2024 problems. After each problem the strongest member runs a debrief and rewrites the strategy: who speaks in which phase, who checks, who argues with the majority, how to reach a conclusion. A bank of 10 strategies was frozen and applied to new problems. A second team, Gemini-2.5-Flash, Llama-4-Maverick and GPT-4.1, learned the same way on knowledge tasks.

On five mathematics and physics benchmarks the team solves 66.7% of problems on average.

- The best model alone gets 48.8%.
- The same model at roughly the same compute budget, called linearization because one model serially plays every role, gets 58.7%.
- A routing oracle that always picks the best of the members' answers gets 59.0%.
- Mixture of Agents gets 57.3%.
- Three copies of o3-mini with the same strategies get 56.0%.

On benchmarks newer than the models the picture splits.

- AIME 2026, released after all three models, gives the team 71.2%: 29.0 pp above the best model and 13.4 pp above the routing oracle, but only 2.7 pp above linearization (68.5%).
- HMMT 2026 is a tie at 39.4%.
- On knowledge tasks the gain almost vanishes, 72.8% vs 72.1% for linearization, and the team sits below the routing oracle (79.6%) even though a correct candidate exists for 87.9% of problems.

Generation outruns selection. The easier it is to tell correct reasoning from incorrect, the larger the gain: ρ = 0.90 across 8 benchmarks (p = 0.005).

The organization is learned and still flexible. The authors say the strategies do not prescribe the subtasks of a new problem. The division of reasoning forms, is contested and changes during the discussion. The team beats hand-designed protocols, and nobody specified the roles it invented, a "consensus challenger" and a "final auditor". The design that remains is real. The three-model roster is fixed in advance, the strongest member picks the final answer, and the conversation rules were learned on examples with known answers rather than found during the new task. On some benchmarks the team barely beats one model at the same budget. Model heterogeneity is a noticeable part of the gain (Chapter 8). The code is not released. The models are from the 2025 generation, chosen to "preserve headroom", and the effect is untested on 2026 flagships. The team always has three members. Tokens, cost and time are not reported. The linearization budget is matched only "approximately", and the team makes on the order of 80–110 model calls per problem (our estimate).

:::takeaways
- Structure emerges in a swarm of equals: interface agreements and self-nominated "integrators" in [Agensh](#w-agensh-1024), 2,005 task "claims" in git for the [C compiler](#w-anthropic-c-compiler), two tiers with 12 "sub-leads" among 100 [Opus 5.5](#w-opus55-card) agents; also [AI Village](#w-ai-village) and OpenAI's agent organization, which ended in an [incident](#w-hf-incident-case).
- In several controlled comparisons, peer teams underperformed alternatives or offered no gain: a team without roles does not [beat its best member](#w-teams-hold-experts-back), a [pair loses 41%](#w-cooperbench) of solo capability, [one agent per user](#w-worse-together) loses to a coordinator, a [human scheme](#w-orch-embodied) beats "emerging leadership".
- Without a human architect, organization wins at comparable cost when an outer loop (training or debriefs) sets its rules in advance: [Self-Organizing Agent Teams](#w-sat-teams), 66.7% vs 58.7% for one model at roughly the same budget; see also [Conductor](#w-sakana-conductor-trinity-fugu).
- Equal agents divide work where the task splits into independent pieces with a checkable success criterion (the [C compiler](#w-anthropic-c-compiler) kernel build was split by file), and fail where work needs trust in others' reports, end-to-end ownership and deference to an expert.
- Open: no one has tested a large swarm of equals against a designed team on one task at equal token budget. The closest, the [Opus 5.5 card](#w-opus55-card), uses only five agents: peers beat a lead with subagents on speed and tokens, with equal final quality.
:::

## For comparison: physical swarms

The same collective dynamics, phase transitions, majority-following, order without a centre, reappear in physical bodies that have neither language nor prompts. Three 2026 papers in Nature Physics, Science Advances and Nature Communications demonstrated them on tens to thousands of devices. For agent swarms they are a reference, not an analogy to stretch: the count of bodies is exact, and Zakir et al. measured one effect from a mathematical model through to hardware.
