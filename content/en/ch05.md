If identical agents get a shared channel — a mailbox, a board, git — but no assigned roles, can they divide the work? And is such an organization better than a designed scheme or a single agent at an equal budget (here and below: equal tokens or compute, not equal time)? The emergence of structure and its advantage are different results, and the chapter treats them separately: first, observations of equal agents from their logs; then the engineering experience of companies, controlled comparisons and learned organization; at the end, a summary table of comparison conditions and, for contrast, physical swarms. The papers in this chapter use the word "self-organization" for three different mechanisms.

:::callout Three different kinds of "self-organization"
The works in this chapter use one word for three different things, and the conclusions differ.

1. **Emergence on the fly.** Equal agents with a shared channel divide the work themselves, during the task: Agensh, Carlini's compiler, AutoScientists, AI Village (Chapter 10). This is self-organization in the pure sense.
2. **Learned organization.** An outer loop works out the rules of collaboration before the work starts, and they are then frozen: Self-Organizing Agent Teams (a debrief after each task), Sakana's Conductor and TRINITY (reinforcement learning and evolutionary search). No human draws the scheme, but equal agents do not invent it from scratch either: at Sakana a trained dispatcher picks the workers and roles for each task, and in Self-Organizing Agent Teams the frozen strategies set the order of the conversation, while the division of work on a new task forms and changes as the work proceeds.
3. **Designed through iteration.** Engineers read failure logs and redraw the scheme: Cursor, Anthropic Labs, Cognition. The authors sometimes call the result "emergent", but that is their interpretation, not an experiment.
:::

## Equal agents with a shared channel: what the logs show

This section collects runs in which many identical agents get a shared channel, a task and time, while humans stay only in the outer loop. Two such runs with open data appeared in 2026: a thousand Microsoft Research agents on one program and sixteen Anthropic agents on a compiler; a third observation, without open logs, comes from the Claude Opus 5.5 system card. All three show that structure emerges; none compares it with a designed one.

:::card agensh-1024
lead: Up to 1,024 identical agents with no dispatcher hold together and put forward roles on their own; the pandoc pass rate rises from 33.89% (one agent) to 55.06%. Time (6 h) was equalized, not compute.
**What they did.** The authors gave one large programming task to an "organization" of identical agents with no dispatcher. Every worker is the same model, GPT-5.6-sol, in the Copilot harness with the same prompt, which differs only in the worker ID; the agents share a Gitea Git server, a Mattermost chat and a board for claims, findings and failures. The task comes from ProgramBench: in 6 hours, without Internet access, rebuild an existing program (FFmpeg, gromacs, pandoc, PHP-src or ctags) with nothing but the ability to run it. Teams of 1, 8, 32 and 128 agents ran on all tasks, and 1,024 agents on pandoc only.

**Results.**

- **Mean over five tasks** (share of hidden tests passed): 19.31% with one agent, 20.68% with 8, 26.52% with 32, 28.78% with 128 (+9.47 pp).
- **pandoc:** 33.89 → 41.75 → 43.11 → 50.94 → 55.06% for 1, 8, 32, 128 and 1,024 agents, about +2 pp per doubling (our estimate).
- **Speed:** on pandoc, 128 agents exceeded 30% by minute 30; the single agent did not get there in two hours.
- **Cooperation in the logs** grows more complex with team size: from agreements on interfaces at 8 agents to reviewers chosen by experience, a negotiated integration protocol and self-nominated "integrators" at 128 and 1,024.
- **Replay of the 1,024-agent run** (our count): 11,111 pull requests, 2,800 merged (25%); 148,079 board entries, 56,472 of them FAIL.
- **The curves are jumpy:** on ctags, 8 agents do worse than one (18.47% vs 25.82%); on PHP-src, 128 agents fell from 28.47% in the second hour to 12.35% at the finish, with no explanation; pandoc and ctags supply almost all of the gain.

**Why it matters.** The setup isolates the mechanism: identical prompts, no dispatcher, a shared channel of messages, board and git; with a thousand participants, roles appear without being assigned. There is no comparison with a designed hierarchy: the authors' claim that the orchestrator is the bottleneck rests on reasoning, not measurement.

**Caveats.** Matched on time, not compute: tokens were not counted; the 128- and 1,024-agent teams got roughly 117 and 800 times the agent-hours of one agent (our estimate); and the single agent was still improving at hour six and only kept working for all 6 hours thanks to a "stop hook". No repeats are reported, and the prompt prescribes a detailed work cycle ("no structure" means "no boss"); preprint; the code is unavailable (404).

:::card anthropic-c-compiler
lead: In two weeks, 16 Claude Opus 4.6 copies with no boss or messaging wrote a C compiler that builds a bootable Linux kernel; parallelism broke where the task was indivisible. One run, no control.
**What they did.** The author ran 16 copies of Claude Opus 4.6, each in its own container with a clone of one shared git repository, and tasked them with writing, in Rust, a C compiler that builds the Linux kernel. There was no boss and the agents did not message each other: an agent "claimed" a task with a lock file in git, and on a double claim the push conflict forced the second agent to pick something else; notes and a queue of ideas also lived in files. Every session started with an empty context.

**Results.** By the author's account: nearly 2,000 sessions over two weeks, 2 billion input and 140 million output tokens, just under $20,000; the "100,000-line" compiler builds a bootable Linux 6.9 kernel for x86, ARM and RISC-V, compiles QEMU, SQLite, PostgreSQL and Doom, and passes 99% of the tests in most test suites. From the repository history (our count): 3,982 commits from 23 January to 5 February 2026, 2,005 "claims", 14 team restarts, 186.7 thousand lines of Rust. While building the kernel was one indivisible task, all 16 agents hit the same bug, wrote the same fix and overwrote each other. What helped was a scheme devised by the human: a reference GCC compiles some of the files and the new compiler the rest, so the task splits into independent pieces.

**Why it matters.** A two-week run of equal agents on a real project with an open git history: the agents choose their tasks themselves and carry the project to a result. The limits of self-organization are visible in the same run: the five specialist roles (deduplication, speed, efficiency of the output code, architecture critic, documentation) were assigned by the human, who also designed the tests, the CI and the reference scheme and restarted the team about 14 times.

**Caveats.** One run with no control: no single agent at the same budget, no other team size; the prompt, tests and harness are unpublished. The compiler is weaker than claimed: the author himself admits that GCC compiles the 16-bit x86 boot code, the compiler's own assembler and linker are rough, the code it generates is slower than GCC's with optimizations disabled, and towards the end new changes broke old ones — the limit of the model. Independent checks: John Regehr found 34 miscompilation bugs; SPEC CPU2017 programs showed on average more than 70% lower performance than when built with GCC; and some of the bugs match bugs of the hobby compiler chibicc.

The review's chart from repository data, not an Anthropic illustration. | Source: our analysis of the anthropics/claudes-c-compiler repository history (data CC0-1.0).
:::

The third observation comes from the Claude Opus 5.5 system card (Anthropic, 22 September 2026; the economics of these teams are covered in Chapter 4): a hundred agents build a hierarchy themselves, and the task sets its shape. Teams of 1, 10, 30 and 100 agents worked for 24 hours on one machine on two tasks: building a knowledge base about a company and proving a theorem in Lean. The harness designated only the lead; who messaged whom and who handed out work to whom, the agents decided themselves. On the Lean task the 100-agent team formed two tiers: the lead stayed in contact with 12 "sub-leads", each of which handed out work to its own group of 3–9 helpers and reported back, while five more helpers reported to the lead directly ("sub-lead" is the authors' post-hoc definition: a helper that is the main sender of messages to at least three other helpers). On the knowledge base the same hundred stayed flat: the lead split up the records in advance, and the helpers barely talked to each other. The structure was read from the transcripts of one 100-agent team per task; the card does not measure any gain of the two-tier scheme over the flat one, and team size, as shown in Chapter 4, adds almost nothing to the result beyond 10 agents.

:::short autoscientists
lead: A team with no central planner beats a single-agent loop, but at 4.4× the money.
Marinka Zitnik's group (Harvard) runs long scientific searches on Claude Sonnet 4.6: by default 6 experimenters and 3 analysts; the teams discuss directions, vote and reorganize when a direction stalls. On 24 BioML-Bench tasks the mean leaderboard percentile is 74.40 vs 66.07 for the single-agent Autoresearch loop; starting from an already strong champion on training a small GPT, the team accepted 7 improvements out of 93 attempts, the single agent 0 out of 100. Self-organization is limited here: roles are set at launch, and team membership after a vote is recorded by one agent, the alphabetically last analyst. Tokens cost about 4.4 times as much ($3,985 vs $910), and a 14-agent team is worse than a smaller one on every task of the size check. Preprint.
:::

:::short mollick-dot-and-swarm
lead: A popular-essay version of the idea that agents no longer need to be assigned roles in advance; in his Navier–Stokes example, according to OpenAI's page, people steered the swarm's work.
Ethan Mollick's post of 1 October 2026 argues that a swarm divides up the work by itself. His example is the Navier–Stokes solution (chronicle in Chapter 2, economics in Chapter 4): according to OpenAI's page, the result came from a group of "on the order of 10,000 concurrent agents" in about 88 hours, and the whole Navier–Stokes effort took 2.7 million messages and about 130 billion output tokens; Mollick writes "thousands" of agents. The same OpenAI page describes the human role: people chose the problem, moved agents onto it from other problems, prompted them with the Euler result and cross-pollinated groups via Codex. That is thinner than an org chart but thicker than "no one organized them". The Clay prize has not been awarded; this is a blog, not a measurement.
:::

## Engineering experience: flat swarm vs hierarchy

Companies that run swarms on real codebases reached similar conclusions independently of each other: a swarm of equals did not work for them; a scheme drawn by people did. Cursor described this path in three posts: it started with exactly a swarm of equals and redrew the scheme from failure logs.

:::card cursor-scaling
lead: The flat "swarm of equals" failed: 20 agents worked at the throughput of one to three. A hierarchy of planners and workers, found by engineers from the logs, worked. Company posts, no control.
**What they did.** Three posts (14 January, 5 February and 20 July 2026) describe how Cursor ran hundreds of coding agents that spent weeks writing one project without humans: a browser in Rust from scratch, then SQLite in Rust from the documentation alone. The first version was literally a "swarm of equals": equal status and a shared coordination file with locks, then optimistic concurrency control and a "wait for another agent" tool.

**Results.**

- **The flat scheme failed quickly:** twenty agents worked at the throughput of two or three (first post) or one to three (second). Agents held locks too long, forgot to release them or used them incorrectly; dropping locks cut the overhead, but with no hierarchy the agents became risk-averse, made small safe changes, and nobody took responsibility for hard end-to-end tasks.
- **What worked was a scheme found over four design generations from the logs:** a root planner, recursive sub-planners with narrow areas, and workers on their own copies of the repository who each do one task without coordinating with anyone and hand in a written report; the central integrator was removed as a bottleneck.
- **Browser:** a peak of about 1,000 commits per hour, a week without intervention; the public repository has 29,858 commits and 1.74 million lines of Rust in the main src folder.
- **SQLite in 4 hours:** the new swarm version passed 73–85% of hidden tests vs 11–77% for the old one; with Grok 4.5 the old one ran away — more than 70,000 merge conflicts, one file edited by 1,173 agents — and was stopped before the two-hour mark, while the new one had fewer than 1,000 conflicts.

**Why it matters.** The swarm of equals was tested on a real project with three coordination variants (locks, optimistic concurrency control, a wait tool), and it did not work; the working hierarchy was designed by people. The word "emergent" in the second post is the authors' interpretation, not an experiment. The cost of the runs is covered in Chapter 4.

**Caveats.** Company posts about its own system: no paper, no harness code, no logs, no comparison with one agent at the same budget; the number of attempts per configuration is not given, and the authors ask readers to look at trends. The flat scheme was tried only briefly, on the models of winter 2025/26 and with three mechanisms; the browser did not build at the time of publication, and a fixed version was posted 2–3 days later.
!fig cursor-scaling_fig1.png | The failed flat scheme: four equal agents and one shared coordination file through which they all try to coordinate. | Source: © Cursor, Wilson Lin, "Towards self-driving codebases", cursor.com/blog (2026), diagram 1; quoted for review.
!fig cursor-scaling_fig2.png | The final scheme: the planner delegates to sub-planners (recursively) and to workers; all workers push to a shared Git. | Source: © Cursor, Wilson Lin, "Towards self-driving codebases", cursor.com/blog (2026), diagram 4; quoted for review.
:::

:::short cognition-multi-agents
lead: Cognition's experience: parallel writing by several agents does not work; a narrow class of schemes does, in which agents contribute intelligence but one agent writes. Vendor opinion without tables.
Walden Yan (Cognition, makers of Devin) advised in June 2025 against building multi-agent systems: parallel "writers" make implicit decisions that conflict, and one linear agent with full context is more reliable. In April 2026 he confirms that this still holds for parallel writing and names the schemes that do work: the Devin Review reviewer, starting from a clean context, finds on average 2 bugs per PR (about 58% of them severe), and pairing Claude and GPT helps in the hardest cases. He calls unstructured swarms mostly a distraction, names "map-reduce-and-manage" as the practical shape, and notes that messaging between agents does not emerge by itself, because the models were not trained for it. The numbers come with no sample size or baseline.
:::

:::short harness-design-long-running
lead: On one prompt, a three-agent harness with assigned roles built a playable game maker where solo Opus 4.5 did not, but took 6 hours and $200 vs 20 minutes and $9, from an expanded spec.
Prithvi Rajasekaran (Anthropic Labs, 24 March 2026) built three agents with assigned roles: a planner, a generator and an evaluator that clicks through the app with Playwright. In the only direct comparison described in detail — one prompt for a retro game maker — a solo Claude Opus 4.5 worked 20 minutes for $9 and the harness 6 hours for $200; in the solo version the entities did not respond to input, while in the harness version one could control a character and play (although the author got stuck at one wall). But the task was not the same: the planner itself expanded the one-sentence prompt into 16 features. The author's lesson is a different one: every harness component is an assumption about what the model cannot yet do alone, and it is removed as soon as a newer model manages on its own (with Opus 4.6 the sprints disappeared; the planner and the evaluator stayed, evaluation moved to the end of the run, and, according to the author, the evaluator pays off on tasks beyond what the model does reliably solo). One run, no benchmark.
:::

The [Anthropic campaign](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-discovers-novel-enzyme-system) in which agents searched protein databases for new enzyme systems (23 September 2026; analysed in detail in Chapter 6) is likewise organized from the top. The "roughly 950 agents" of the announcement are 949 Claude Mythos 5 sessions over 21.5 hours, with at most 58 able to run at the same time. The roles of worker, supervisor, "curator" of the shared knowledge base and editor were set in advance, and the five stages ran as a chain: the next opened only when a script accepted the previous one. Initiative also came from below — the agents opened 98 of the 119 tasks for themselves — but neither a flat team nor a single agent was tried on the same task, so this is an example of practice, not a comparison.

## Controlled comparisons: equal agents vs a coordinator, a designed scheme and a single agent

Engineering stories have no control. Controlled studies that compare equal agents with a communication channel against a single agent or a given structure became numerous in 2026, and almost all give the same answer: a swarm of equals beats neither a coordinator, nor a drawn scheme, nor a single agent doing the same amount of work.

:::card teams-hold-experts-back
lead: With no expert named, a team without roles does not beat its best member on any of five benchmarks; on ranking tasks non-experts average the expert's view with theirs, and the gap grows with size.
**What they did.** The authors tested a pure swarm of equals: several different models with no roles or rules discuss a task for four rounds and give a joint answer. The teams were deliberately built from one strong and several weak models (e.g., GPT-5 with GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Haiku). Five AI benchmarks and three psychology ranking tasks in which one or several members hold the knowledge.

**Results.**

- **Expert not named:** the team did not beat its best model on any benchmark: 86.0% vs 86.5% (MMLU-Pro), 76 vs 78 (GPQA), 51 vs 52 (SimpleQA), 28 vs 29 (HLE), 63 vs 73.5 (MATH-500); it does beat the average member and simple voting.
- **Ceiling:** if the team picked, on every question, the answer of whoever was right, it would score 91.8 / 88.8 / 62.3 / 47.5 / 79.0%; the shortfall from that ceiling is 6.3% to 41.1% (relative).
- **Expert named** (no such hint exists in practice): the team beat the best model on four benchmarks of five but did not reach the ceiling.
- **Psychology tasks:** the team's error is 17–113% higher than the expert's, and members do not defer even to a named expert: non-experts treat the expert's view as something to average with their own ("integrative compromise"; r = 0.55 and 0.69 with the loss, significant on two tasks of three), and experts give in.
- **Team size:** the larger the team (2 → 4 → 8), the larger the gap (r = 0.32–0.59).
- **Controls:** four experts together do about as well as one, so the cause is unequal knowledge, not conversational noise; one saboteur barely hurts (26.0 vs 26.7).

**Why it matters.** A controlled test of the hope that equal agents will organize themselves around the strongest member: they average it out, and the authors recommend explicit roles. The paper was accepted at ICML 2026. The next paper by the same first and senior authors is about learned organization (below).

**Caveats.** The mechanism was measured on cheap 2024 models (Claude 3.5 Haiku, GPT-4o-mini); GPT-5, Opus 4.5 and Sonnet 4.5 take part only in the benchmark part, with 100 questions per benchmark and no confidence intervals, so differences of 0.5–2 pp may be chance. The format is short — one question, four rounds, no tools or memory; long-running swarms were not tested; the team makes 25–28 model calls per task instead of one (our estimate from the protocol), tokens are not reported, and the explanation through training for agreeableness is the authors' hypothesis.

:::short cooperbench
lead: The "curse of coordination": a pair of agents loses, on average across five models, 41% of single-agent capability.
Stanford and SAP Labs built 652 tasks: two agents get different features that overlap in the code; their changes are then merged and the tests for both features are run. GPT-5 succeeds as a pair 0.28 of the time vs 0.48 when one agent gets both features; Claude Sonnet 4.5, 0.26 vs 0.47; MiniMax-M2, 0.14 vs 0.36. Chat reduces merge conflicts but does not raise success, and in an experiment on 46 tasks growing the team from 2 to 3 and 4 agents drops success from 68.6% to 46.5% and 30.0%. Role division emerges by itself, but rarely; the failure causes are a wrong picture of the partner (42% of the 50 analysed), broken commitments (32%) and failed exchanges (26%). Pairs, not swarms; preprint.
:::

:::short worse-together
lead: A team with one agent per user is worse than a single coordinator in every environment, and a mailbox does not close the gap.
Paliskara, Namchittai and Lampinen, in a preprint of 30 September 2026, describe 77 scenarios in four environments where each user has their own agent but the resource is shared: an API budget, a merge queue, a clinic, a personal assistant (a joint order). On the API budget, peer-to-peer gets 30% of the optimum with Claude Opus 5 and 12% with Sonnet 5 vs 64% and 32% for a coordinator, and without a communication channel 7% and 2%. As the team grows from 4 to 16 agents, the share of agents that act falls from 66% to 10% with Sonnet 5, and in the clinic a silent Opus 5 team beats a communicating one (81.0% vs 69.5%). The gap is closed by an appointed lead, a strict instruction, or blocking an order until the other agents' messages have been read — an assigned role, not a hierarchy built by the agents. The benchmark has not been released yet.
:::

:::short when-agents-coordinate
lead: The task, not the agents, sets the shape of the communication network, and the word "coordinator" in the prompt changes nothing.
Destefanis and Aste recorded 1,902 runs of teams of 1 to 16 Claude Sonnet 4.6 agents in Claude Code on two synthetic Python tasks, plus 244 runs in a sealed environment. The agents do build a network: a shared specification produces a dense network (mean degree 5.47 at 8 agents), a pipeline a sparse one (2.99), and at 16 agents direct links nearly vanish (0.28). The phrase "you are the coordinator" creates neither a hub nor a gain: in the sealed replication there is no significant difference between a flat team and a team with a "coordinator" (20/30 vs 21/32, p = 1.00). An eight-step chain passes in 9 of 10 runs with two and four agents and in 0 of 10 with eight, when a rounding rule falls on the boundary between two owners. One model, and large variance between sessions.
:::

:::short orch-embodied
lead: A hierarchy drawn by a human beats both a scheme drawn by a model and dynamic leadership.
A Duke University group ran teams of up to 50 heterogeneous agents in the CREW-Wildfire firefighting simulator on 25 missions, eight models and five seeds; the ORCH scheme is drawn before the start and not changed during the mission. Per the abstract, the human scheme gives +63.97% in score and +74.29% in efficiency relative to four earlier multi-agent algorithms, and the model-drawn scheme +43.63% and +52.53%; raw means are not given in the text. Episode success rate: 53.1% for the human scheme, 42.2% for the model with a critic, 35.2% without a critic, 6.9–13.5% for the baseline systems, including CAMON with its "emerging leadership" (1,000 runs each). Simulation; no comparison with a single agent; the error analysis was done by a language model.
:::

:::short dochkina-drop-hierarchy
lead: An external turn order with self-selected roles beats both a central coordinator and fully simultaneous work.
Victoria Dochkina compared coordination protocols for groups of 4 to 256 agents on synthetic tasks; quality is judged by another model. The winning protocol, Sequential, keeps an external turn order, and the agents choose only their role: on GPT-4.1-mini (8 agents), 0.724 vs 0.640 for a central coordinator and 0.503 for fully simultaneous Shared (+44%, Cohen's d 1.86). On Claude Sonnet 4.6, DeepSeek v3.2 and GLM-5 (16 agents, judge GPT-5.4), Sequential beats the coordinator by 14.1%, 12.4% and 12.2%, and Shared was not re-run. From 64 to 256 agents quality does not change (about 0.96); the agents invent roles anew almost every time (5,006 unique names), and at 256 agents about 45% withdraw on their own. Single author, no data released; the 25,000 runs stated in the abstract do not match the table (about 20,810).
:::

A counterexample in favour of equals comes from the Claude Opus 5.5 system card (§8.12.1–8.12.2; more in Chapter 4). There, a team of five peer agents — each with the full task statement, identical tools and messaging — was compared with a scheme in which a lead spawns subagents itself and they see only its instructions. On ProgramBench (measured on "an alternate, broadly comparable snapshot" of the model), the peer five reached 60% of hidden tests faster — about 38 vs 66 minutes of derived latency — and spent fewer tokens to get there, about 1.1 vs 2 million (both pairs of numbers are our estimates from the charts), while the final ceiling is the same for both schemes and for the single agent. On DRACO under a tight time budget, the lead spawns subagents much less often and prefers to work alone, while a pre-assembled team keeps dividing the work. The gain here is in speed and tokens, not in final quality, and both schemes were designed by people, but in this comparison the flat team of equals did not lose to the hierarchy.

## Learned organization: an outer loop selects the rules

If equal agents do not find a good structure on the fly, and a scheme drawn by people quickly goes stale, a third option remains: a separate loop selects the rules of organization in advance — reinforcement learning or a "debrief" after each task. This is not a swarm of equals: no human draws the scheme, but the agents do not invent it as they work either; only the division of labour adapts to the specific task (the second kind in the box at the start of the chapter).

:::card sat-teams
lead: The learned organization of three different models beats both a single model and hand-designed protocols (66.7% vs 58.7% for one model at roughly the same budget), but on some benchmarks the gain almost vanishes.
**What they did.** The first and senior authors of "Teams Hold Experts Back", with colleagues, tried to teach a team to reason together from its own experience. Three different models — o3-mini, Claude Sonnet 4 and DeepSeek-V3 — solve 15 AIME 2024 problems; after each problem the strongest member runs a debrief and rewrites the strategy: who speaks in which phase, who checks, who argues with the majority, how to reach a conclusion. A bank of 10 strategies was frozen and applied to new problems; a second team (Gemini-2.5-Flash, Llama-4-Maverick, GPT-4.1) learned the same way on "knowledge" tasks.

**Results.**

- **Math and physics, five benchmarks:** the team solves 66.7% of problems on average. The best model alone gets 48.8%; the same model at roughly the same compute budget ("linearization": one model serially plays every role) 58.7%; the routing oracle (always picking the best of the members' answers) 59.0%; Mixture of Agents 57.3%; three copies of o3-mini with the same strategies 56.0%.
- **Benchmarks newer than the models:** on AIME 2026, released after all three models, the team scores 71.2%: 29.0 pp above the best model and 13.4 pp above the routing oracle, but only 2.7 pp above linearization (68.5%); on HMMT 2026 it is a tie (39.4%).
- **"Knowledge" tasks:** the gain almost vanishes: 72.8% vs 72.1% for linearization, below the routing oracle (79.6%), although a correct candidate exists for 87.9% of problems: generation outruns selection.
- **When the gain is larger:** the easier it is to tell correct reasoning from incorrect, the larger the gain: ρ = 0.90 across 8 benchmarks (p = 0.005).

**Why it matters.** The organization is learned but flexible: according to the authors, the strategies do not prescribe the subtasks of a new problem, and the division of reasoning forms, is contested and changes as the discussion goes. What comes from self-organization: the team beats hand-designed protocols, and nobody specified the roles it invented (a "consensus challenger", a "final auditor"). What comes from design: the three-model roster is fixed in advance (the strongest member picks the final answer), the conversation rules were learned on examples with known answers rather than found by the agents on the fly, and on some benchmarks the team barely beats one model with the same budget. Model heterogeneity is a noticeable part of the gain (Chapter 8).

**Caveats.** Code not released; the models are of the 2025 generation, chosen to "preserve headroom"; the effect is untested for 2026 flagships, and the team always has three members. Tokens, cost and time are not reported; the linearization budget is matched only "approximately", and the team makes on the order of 80–110 model calls per problem (our estimate).

:::short sakana-conductor-trinity-fugu
lead: Sakana's small trained "conductors" choose which LLM handles a task and in which role; Conductor scores 77.27 vs 74.78 for the best single model (GPT-5).
Sakana AI trained small "conductor" models that choose which of other providers' LLMs to hand a task to, and in which role. Conductor (7B, reinforcement learning, a plan of at most 5 steps) averages 77.27 across 7 benchmarks vs 74.78 for the best single model (GPT-5); in a controlled setting, 72.35 vs 67.60 for a single model with five times the context. TRINITY (a 0.6B coordinator, roles Thinker / Worker / Verifier) gives 70.44 vs 61.12–65.95 when the same protocol runs on a single model. Fugu-Ultra, on top of Gemini 3.1 Pro, Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5, scores 73.7 on SWE-Bench Pro vs 69.2 for Opus 4.8. No human draws the topology, but equal agents do not invent it either: a central orchestrator, at most five steps. The competitor numbers for Fugu are taken from vendors and leaderboards.

:::short evans-agentic-intelligence-explosion
lead: A conceptual article in Science: the "intelligence explosion" will be a society of agents that self-organizes at every level, but within institutions designed by people. No data.
James Evans, Benjamin Bratton and Blaise Agüera y Arcas (Google, University of Chicago, Santa Fe Institute, Berggruen Institute) argue in a short article in Science (19 March 2026) that the "intelligence explosion" will be not one giant mind but a society of agents. They describe recursive societies in which an agent facing a hard task spawns specialized copies, and those spawn their own sub-societies: self-organization at every level. But they also call for designing institutions, role protocols and constructive conflict as deliberate features (devil's advocacy, brainstorming), and for replacing dyadic alignment through RLHF with institutional alignment, with humans in the loop. The anchor is the authors' own preprint on "societies of thought" inside reasoning models, plus an analogy with past evolutionary transitions. Conceptual support for the idea, not a measurement.
:::

## Summary: what was compared with what

The main reason the works disagree is that they compare different things and equalize different things. The table collects the control conditions.

| Work | Agents | What vs what | Equalized on | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Agensh](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26781) | 1–1,024 identical | swarm of equals vs single agent | time (6 h), not tokens | structure emerges; quality rises with N: 128 agents give +9.47 pp across five tasks at ~117× the agent-hours; ~800× applies only to the 1,024-agent pandoc run (agent-hours are upper bounds, our estimate) |
| [C compiler](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler) | 16 identical | no control | — | structure emerges; roles assigned by a human |
| [Opus 5.5 card, §8.12.3](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/fc1b44717c85dc068bc6ba5024219938094694bd/Claude%20Opus%205.5%20System%20Card.pdf) | 1–100 identical | team sizes against each other; structure without a control | time (24 h) | structure emerges (two tiers on Lean, flat on the knowledge base); beyond 10 agents the returns to size are small |
| Cursor: two posts from the three-post series | hundreds | flat swarm (20 agents) vs hierarchy — ["Towards self-driving codebases"](https://cursor.com/blog/self-driving-codebases); old hierarchy vs new — ["Agent swarms and the new model economics"](https://cursor.com/blog/agent-swarm-model-economics) | flat: not equalized; versions: time (4 h) | the flat swarm failed: 20 agents worked at the throughput of 1–3; the hierarchy was found by people |

:::takeaways
- Structure emerges in a swarm of equals: interface agreements and self-nominated "integrators" in [Agensh](#w-agensh-1024), 2,005 task "claims" in git for the [C compiler](#w-anthropic-c-compiler), two tiers with 12 "sub-leads" among 100 [Opus 5.5](#w-opus55-card) agents; also [AI Village](#w-ai-village) and OpenAI's agent organization, which ended in an [incident](#w-hf-incident-case).
- In several controlled comparisons, peer teams underperformed alternatives or offered no gain: a team without roles does not [beat its best member](#w-teams-hold-experts-back), a [pair loses 41%](#w-cooperbench) of solo capability, [one agent per user](#w-worse-together) loses to a coordinator, a [human scheme](#w-orch-embodied) beats "emerging leadership".
- Without a human architect, organization wins at comparable cost when an outer loop (training or debriefs) sets its rules in advance: [Self-Organizing Agent Teams](#w-sat-teams), 66.7% vs 58.7% for one model at roughly the same budget; see also [Conductor](#w-sakana-conductor-trinity-fugu).
- Equal agents divide work where the task splits into independent pieces with a checkable success criterion (the [C compiler](#w-anthropic-c-compiler) kernel build was split by file), and fail where work needs trust in others' reports, end-to-end ownership and deference to an expert.
- Open: no one has tested a large swarm of equals against a designed team on one task at equal token budget. The closest, the [Opus 5.5 card](#w-opus55-card), uses only five agents: peers beat a lead with subagents on speed and tokens, with equal final quality.
:::

## For comparison: physical swarms

The same collective dynamics — phase transitions, following the majority, order without a centre — reappear in physical bodies that have neither language nor prompts. Three 2026 papers in Nature Physics, Science Advances and Nature Communications demonstrated them on tens to thousands of physical devices.

| Work | Who | Scale | What they showed |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Phase coherence and disorder-induced wave propagation in micromotor arrays](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41567-026-03409-x) | ENS de Lyon, CNRS, LPENSL (Braun, Poncet, Bartolo); Leiden University (Morin); Nature Physics, 11 August 2026; preprint [arXiv:2604.07080](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07080), April 2026 | a 48 × 44 = 2,112 lattice of Quincke motors | thousands of 3D-printed rotating motors, given neither a pattern nor a direction, settle by themselves into a clean antiferromagnetic phase with phase coherence of their precession; introduced disorder triggers free propagation of phase waves across the lattice |
| [Fluidic torque–enabled object manipulation by microrobot collectives](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aea9947) | University of Michigan; Max Planck Institute for Intelligent Systems, Stuttgart; Cornell University; Koç University (Ceron, Gardi, Petersen, Sitti); Science Advances, 27 February 2026 | a dense collective of 1,000 magnetic disks of Ø 300 µm and 20 passive objects of Ø 1 mm | a collective of microrobots manipulates foreign objects through hydrodynamic (fluidic) torque |
| [Bio-inspired decision making in robot swarms under biases](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-76408-4) | IRIDIA, Université Libre de Bruxelles (Zakir, Dorigo); naXys, University of Namur (Carletti); University of Konstanz and Max Planck Institute of Animal Behavior (Reina); Nature Communications, 14 August 2026 | 50 Kilobot robots in 24 experiments; 100 robots in the ARGoS simulator; a Gillespie model at N = 50, 100, 200 and 400 | under "asocial" interference (stubborn robots, self-sourced information, corrupted messages) a cross-inhibition rule borrowed from living swarms yields fast, consistent and robust collective choice, while direct switching gets stuck in deadlock; tested at three levels: physical robots, simulator, mathematical model |

For swarms of AI agents this is a reference point: the same collective dynamics arise in bodies with no model and no language. It is also a bar for method: the scale (50, 1,000, 2,112 bodies) is known exactly, and Zakir et al. measured one effect all the way from a mathematical model to hardware.
