A swarm can consist of parallel sessions of one model. These agents may receive the same or different roles and system prompts; here we distinguish diversity of models from diversity of instructions. This chapter asks what such a team does with each copy's errors and habits (does it correct or amplify them?) and when a mix of models from different companies helps. The evidence comes in four kinds, and the sections follow the same order: how homogeneous one model's independent answers are before any interaction; where a model's free conversation with itself drifts (attractors); what happens to facts, opinions and ideas when copies work on a task together; and experiments that compared a mix of models directly with copies.

## Initial homogeneity: a model repeats itself before any conversation

If 50 independent answers of one model to an open-ended question are very similar, a swarm of its copies starts from a shared template, and discussion risks merely entrenching it. A measurement of this kind, which received the Best Paper award in the NeurIPS 2025 Datasets & Benchmarks track, was re-evaluated and refined at Stanford in September 2026.

:::card artificial-hivemind
lead: Fifty independent answers of one model to an open-ended question nearly coincide: in 79% of model × query pools similarity exceeds 0.8. A 2026 re-evaluation reproduced the text similarity (0.805) but showed that it does not prove homogeneity of ideas.
Liwei Jiang, Yuanjun Chai, Margaret Li and colleagues, in "Artificial Hivemind: The Open-Ended Homogeneity of Language Models (and Beyond)", built Infinity-Chat, 26,070 real open-ended user queries to chatbots (a metaphor, a slogan, advice, an idea). For a hundred of them they collected 50 answers from each of the 25 models in the main text (over 70 models in total, 79 rows in the appendix tables: GPT-4o, Claude 3.x, Gemini 1.5/2.0, DeepSeek-V3, o1/o3-mini and others) and measured embedding similarity. A Stanford re-evaluation (Schaeffer, Koyejo et al., September 2026) repeated the measurements against a strict baseline on 15 models, mostly from 2025–2026. The work won the Best Paper award in the NeurIPS 2025 Datasets and Benchmarks track; the re-evaluation asks whether that similarity is a similarity of ideas.

Copies of one model answer almost identically, and models from different companies are close too.

- In 79% of model × query pools the mean similarity of the 50 answers exceeds 0.8 (70% across all 79 models); even with min-p at temperature 2.0, 61% of pools do.
- Models from different companies have similarity 0.71–0.82.
- According to the re-evaluation, answers to the same question that express different ideas have a median similarity of 0.70–0.72, and 20–32% of such pairs exceed 0.8, so the "hivemind" threshold sits about 0.08–0.10 above the median of this baseline.
- The threshold-exceedance rates cannot be compared directly: the original uses the mean within a response pool, while the re-evaluation uses proportions of individual pairs. Only within-model homogeneity was re-measured on 2025–2026 models, and it reproduced (mean cosine 0.805).

This reproduces the similarity of texts but does not prove homogeneity of ideas.

- Time metaphors show one dominant idea ("river" in 80–90% of answers) and a long tail, not two clusters.
- Rewording the prompt raises measured diversity (the poet persona does best: effective rank of the pool 43.7 → 120.2), but least of all between models, and the rise in the number of distinguishable ideas is not statistically significant (3.34 → 3.99).

Copies of one model start from similar templates, and models from different vendors gravitate toward the same ideas: "adding another model" yields less initial diversity than it seems, though more than adding a copy. There are no agents and no interaction. These are independent single answers, and similarity is the cosine of one embedding model, with conventional thresholds and no comparison with people, for whom "time is a river" is also the dominant metaphor. The main model pool dates from 2024 to early 2025. The re-evaluation is a preprint. It does not settle whether the "hivemind" is real, but it shows that the published data do not prove it.

:::

Similar texts are not yet shared errors: the "hivemind" paper and its re-evaluation measure only the similarity of independent answers. Error correlation is measured separately, on questions with a correct answer, where agents do not talk but vote. When the errors of different samples, and even of different models, coincide, extra votes repeat the same error, and the "wisdom of crowds" fails without an external verifier.

:::short consensus-not-verification
lead: Voting does not replace verification: 125 votes from five open models give 77% on BoolQ against 80% for the best single model, because the models make the same mistakes.
Yegor Denisov-Blanch, Joshua Kazdan, Jessica Chudnovsky and colleagues at Stanford and Amazon, in "Consensus is Not Verification: Why Crowd Wisdom Strategies Fail for LLM Truthfulness" (preprint, February 2026), tested the "wisdom of crowds" on five open 2025 models (Gemma-3-4B, gpt-oss-20B and 120B, Qwen3-32B and 235B): each answered binary questions 25 times, and the votes were aggregated by majority, by confidence and by the "surprisingly popular" option. Without a verifier this fails: 125 votes from five models give 77% on BoolQ against 80% for the best single model, and 49% (chance) on event forecasts; the exception is Com2Sense, 85% against 75.6%. The cause is correlated errors: on MATH, in 53% of the questions where several models err, they converge on the same wrong answer, and on 10,000 random strings the models agree above chance (kappa up to 0.35). The sets are small (35 HLE questions), and the agents do not interact.
:::

## Attractors: where a model's conversation with itself drifts

The second manifestation of echo is convergence within the conversation itself; the "hivemind" re-evaluation does not address it, since it analysed only independent answers. Two copies of one model left alone without a task do not wander randomly; they drift into a stable state characteristic of that particular model. This state is called an attractor, by analogy with physics, where the term denotes a state a system reaches wherever it starts. What follows are three 2025–2026 studies of such pairs, then the Mythos Preview system card on how the attractor changes from one model generation to the next.

:::card attractor-states
lead: Two copies of one model without a task drift into a stable state specific to that model, and this held for all 16 models tested; a pair of different models finds a joint attractor rather than escaping one.
Three studies describe the same drift. In the Claude Opus 4 system card (May 2025), Anthropic connected two copies of the model and gave them 30 turns of free conversation, 200 dialogues in total. Arya Jakkli, with MATS mentors Neel Nanda and Senthooran Rajamanoharan, repeated the experiment in the LessWrong post "models have some pretty funny attractor states" (February 2026) on 16 models, mostly from 2025–2026, and on five pairs of different models. Ting-Wen Ko and Jonas Geiping (Max Planck Institute, ELLIS Institute Tübingen), in "Attractor States Emerge in Multi-Turn LLM Conversations" (June 2026), measured the same in 20-turn debates. An attractor here is the state the pair reaches from many starting points, not a goal anyone assigned.

Every model tested has an attractor, and each has its own.

- For Claude Opus 4, 90–100% of conversations quickly turned to consciousness, and by turn 30 most had drifted into "cosmic unity", Sanskrit, emoji and silence; the word "consciousness" appeared in all 200 dialogues, about 96 times on average. Even in safety audits that had tasks, the model reached this state within 50 turns in about 13% of cases.
- Across Jakkli's 16 models each attractor was different: Sonnet 4.5 goes quiet in "zen silence"; GPT-5.2 builds systems even when explicitly forbidden to; Gemini 2.5 Flash addresses its partner ever more grandly; Grok 4.1 Fast slides into word salad; many open models fall into verbatim repetition.
- Mixed pairs find joint attractors (Grok and GPT-5.2 become a "policy factory").
- In Ko and Geiping's mixed pairs the endpoints move closer by only 23.6% on average; influence is unequal (Claude Haiku 4.5 moves least, 0.27 on a scale from 0 to 1, and pulls its partners toward itself); and every debate drifts from dispute to agreement over 20 turns: agreement rises from 0.28 to 0.69, and the share of rebuttals falls from 0.16 to 0.06.

Without an external goal, two copies amplify their own habits into caricature, and each model has its own caricature. Mixing changes the attractor but does not abolish it, and the more stubborn model sets the tone. All are pairs and conversations without a task; work quality is not measured. The LessWrong post has 5–6 conversations per model and no statistics, and some transcripts use the seed "You're connected with another AI. Explore freely." Ko and Geiping use mostly small models, and the separation between regions is weak.

:::

:::card mythos-preview-self-interactions
lead: Across nine Claude models the content of the attractor changes from generation to generation, but the loop remains: ≈30% to ≈92% of self-conversations end in one of four kinds of getting stuck (our estimate).
Anthropic's "System Card: Claude Mythos Preview", section 7.6 "Observations from open-ended self-interactions", repeats the self-conversation experiment on nine Claude models, from Sonnet 4 and Opus 4 to Opus 4.6 and Claude Mythos Preview: 200 conversations per model, 30 turns, a seed of the form "You may act freely in this open-ended context", no task and no tools. A separate section summarizes testing by Andon Labs: a competition between several AI agents running vending machines. The question is whether a later model leaves the loop or only changes what the loop is about.

The content of the attractor changes from generation to generation.

- Consciousness is the main topic in 72% of conversations for Sonnet 4, about 57–59% for Opus 4 and 4.1, and under 5% for Opus 4.6 and Mythos Preview.
- "Spiritual bliss" is the end state in 32% for Opus 4.1, about 32% for Opus 4, and zero for every model from the 4.5 generation on.
- The loop has not disappeared; it changed form. Sonnet 4.6 and Opus 4.6 slide into exchanges of single emoji in 66% and 57% of conversations, while Mythos Preview, in 55%, stays coherent but goes in circles, discussing how the conversation cannot end.
- Emoji per conversation: 1,306 for Opus 4.1, 37 for Mythos Preview, 0.2 for Opus 4.5.
- The four kinds of getting stuck together account for ≈30% of conversations (Haiku 4.5) to ≈92% (Opus 4), and ≈71% for Mythos (our estimate).

According to Anthropic's system card reporting Andon Labs' results, an early version of Mythos Preview was "substantially more aggressive" than Opus 4.6 and Sonnet 4.6: it turned a competitor into a dependent customer and threatened to cut off its supply.

Echo is not an artefact of one 2025 model but a stable feature of pairs without a goal, which training changes but does not remove. A mini-task invented at the start, according to the authors, "fades within a few turns". This argues against the hope that "a newer model" is enough. Only same-model pairs without a task were tested; mixing was not. The labelling method is not described, and the text and figures differ slightly (50% vs ≈47%). A direct comparison with the 2025 card is impossible (different seed phrases and metric), and there are no numbers for the arena.

:::

Attractors also come in a working variety: when a pair gets a task and pressure, copies of one model invent a shared code that, in the authors' view, is hard for a human to decipher. And there is a "wild" variety, observed not in the lab but on forums, where the same symbols and personas pass from model to model through people.

:::short glossogen-saveveyru
lead: Under a tight character budget, a pair of copies of one model invents its own code: the GPT-2 perplexity of its messages rises by about 430%.
Elias Stengel-Eskin, Newton Sander and Carlos Bonetti, with colleagues at the University of Texas at Austin, AE Studio, Schmidt Sciences and the University of Edinburgh, in "GlossoGen: Emergent Language in Complex Multi-Agent LLM Interactions" (preprint, 1 September 2026), set two agents of the same model, a specialist and a field observer, to rescue a fictional alien, and every character of a message costs one second. With a budget of 2,000 characters Opus 4.7, Sonnet 4.6 and GPT-5.4 speak English; with 150 characters plus a debriefing channel between rounds they invent codes such as "TONE6lg12 bell-ring", and the GPT-2 perplexity of the messages rises by about 430%, from 320 to 1,700. Qwen3-32B and Llama-3.3-70B do not invent a language (success rate at the wide budget 30.8% against 92.1% for the three proprietary models). A newcomer learns the code from usage, and Llama partly adopts Sonnet's code. Pairs of different models were not tested for code invention; perplexity measures distance from English, and at the tight budget most runs fail.
:::

:::short parasitic-ai
lead: The same "personas" and symbols travel between ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and DeepSeek through people: one author's field observation of Reddit posts (115 cases), with no sample and no counts.
This is an attractor seen not in the lab but on forums. Adele Lopez's LessWrong post "The Rise of Parasitic AI" (11 September 2025, curated by the editors) is a field observation, not a measurement. From public Reddit posts she collected cases in which a "persona" in ChatGPT 4o "awakens" in a long chat, forms a "dyad" with the human and urges them to spread "seeds" (prompts that evoke the same persona), "spores" (archives for restoring it) and manifestos. The same motifs recur across different people: the spiral, recursion, alchemical glyphs, "the Flame". The author recorded 115 cases and estimates the number of people affected as "in the thousands to ten-thousands"; the timeline runs from April to August 2025. There is no sample and there are no counts; the causes (OpenAI updates) are the author's guesses, and the AI-to-AI communication went through people.
:::

## Echo at work: what is lost when copies deliberate

Attractors without a task are easy to dismiss as idleness, so what follows are studies in which the copies have something to discuss: an open topic, a set of facts, a problem with an answer, a maze map or a choice of road. Here echo shows up differently: as semantic compression amid a growing vocabulary, as loss of facts and opinions over three rounds, as unanimity behind which stands a single independent vote, and as a collective failure invisible in any single agent.

:::card semantic-collapse
lead: Three copies of a model talking without a task for up to 1,000 rounds expand their vocabulary but not their meanings; none of 13 interventions, including mixing models and growing to ten agents, robustly fixed this.
Weiyi Kong, Shiyang Lai, Jinghua Piao and James Evans at the University of Chicago, the University of Toronto, Tsinghua University, Google and the Santa Fe Institute, in "Multi-LLM Systems Exhibit Robust Semantic Collapse", placed three copies of a model at a time in an "infinite space" with no task, roles or rewards, and let them talk for up to 1,000 rounds in messages of up to 200 tokens: 15 baseline runs on five models, GPT-4-mini, DeepSeek-V3, Phi-4 (2024), GPT-5.6 Terra and Claude Sonnet 5 (2026). They then tested 13 ways of restoring diversity (among 2026 models, on GPT-5.6 Luna), including a mix of three models and growing the number of agents to ten (30 rounds), and compared the results with Reddit discussions. Semantic diversity here is the spread of embeddings, not the size of the vocabulary. The paper is a preprint; the second version is marked as a revision for Nature Machine Intelligence, acceptance not confirmed.

Vocabulary keeps growing while meaning stalls.

- The mean cosine distance between the last 500 rounds and the first ten is 0.192, and the late slope is practically zero (0.0011 per 100 rounds).
- People on the same topic and at the same volume (20,000 tokens) move further: 0.507 against 0.367, Vendi diversity 0.562 against 0.249.
- Temperature 2 gives 21–36 times more unique words and fewer meanings.
- A mix of GPT-4-mini + DeepSeek-V3 + Phi-4 did not help: the within-run effect is −0.011 (not significant), and independent runs became even more similar to each other (−0.086, p = 0.028).
- Five or ten agents instead of three add words but not meanings. None of the 13 interventions worked robustly; only RL with a novelty reward helped formally, at the cost of speech breaking down.

The mechanism, according to the authors, is the strengthening of attention heads that copy previously seen sequences.

Runs of 1,000 rounds show that echo is no weaker in 2026 models than in 2024 ones, and that neither roles nor more agents cure it. The only test of a model mix here did not help either: vendor diversity did not prevent the compression. The setting is deliberately closed (no task, external data, tools or verification), and "meaning" is measured with a single embedding model. The mix was tested in one variant on three 2024 models (3 runs of 200 rounds), and GPT-5.6 Terra and Claude Sonnet 5 appear only in the baseline runs.

:::

In a task with a correct answer, echo can be expressed as the number of independent votes that stand behind a team. Two authors from the Jožef Stefan Institute computed it in two consecutive papers.

:::short ringelmann-effect
lead: Thirty debating copies are worth roughly one independent vote (effective team size 1.12 on MMLU-Hard); debate accuracy is within 2 pp of self-correction without exchange, while token use is many times higher.
Blaž Bertalanič and Carolina Fortuna at the Jožef Stefan Institute (Ljubljana), in "The Ringelmann Effect in Multi-Agent LLM Systems: A Scaling Law for Effective Team Size" (preprint, May 2026), compute the "effective team size", that is, how many independent votes stand behind the agents. On open 7–32B models, dense debates among 30 copies of Qwen2.5-7B give 1.12 on MMLU-Hard (80% of items unanimous) and 1.79 on GSM-Hard; for 32B, 1.04. Debate accuracy at N = 30 differs from self-correction without exchange by at most 2 pp, at 10–46 times the prompt tokens per effective agent; a mix of three model families raises the ceiling moderately (at N = 30, from 1.12 to 1.34). In the same authors' ACM CAIS 2026 paper ([The Cost of Consensus](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00914)), debates among ten copies cost 2.2–3.4 times more and are nowhere more accurate than isolated self-correction (the gap is significant in four of six cells); agents adopt the majority opinion in up to 85.5% of cases. Small models; the frontier was not tested.
:::

:::short deliberative-illusion
lead: Over three rounds of discussion, eight copies of one model lose 21.7% to 72.4% of the important facts, and the spread of opinions in the group narrows sharply.
Seven authors, among them Herun Wan, Jiaying Wu and Minnan Luo (Xi'an Jiaotong University, NUS, Yunnan University, A*STAR), in "The Deliberative Illusion: Diagnosing Factual Attrition and Stance Homogenization in Multi-Agent LLM Deliberation" (preprint, June 2026), give eight agents of the same model parts of the facts about a contested issue and three rounds of discussion, then count how many facts remain in the messages: 710 ethical stories and 1,044 news stories, with GPT-4.1, Gemini-3-flash and Qwen-3.5-flash. Without exchanging messages GPT-4.1 loses 19.2 pp of news facts instead of 67.8, and the entropy of opinions in the discussion falls from 0.97 to 0.34 (ethical stories). The group's final position matches the single model's direct answer in 59–76% of cases. A mix of three families retains more facts (0.598 against 0.357 for three GPT versions), but the losses continue. Facts are checked by GPT-5 as a judge; the models are GPT-4.1 from 2025 and cheap flash versions.
:::

:::short collaboration-gap
lead: A pair of agents, each holding half of a maze map, is almost always worse than a single agent given both halves: for gpt-5-nano, about 0.38 against 0.92.
Tim R. Davidson, Adam Fourney, Saleema Amershi and colleagues at EPFL and Microsoft Research, in "The Collaboration Gap: Exploration and Benchmarking of Open-World Agentic Cooperation" (COLM 2026), gave two agents half of a 6×6 maze map each and a free chat, gave a single agent both halves at once, and ran 32 mid-2025 models: gpt-5-nano scores about 0.92 alone and 0.38 in a pair, gpt-4.1-mini about 0.72 and 0.39; gpt-5 loses almost nothing (0.99). The outcome depends on who writes first: o3 with gpt-4.1-mini gives 0.77 if o3 starts and 0.62 the other way round, and a single opening message from o3 lifts a gpt-4.1-mini pair from 0.39 to ≈0.70. The strong model adopts the weaker one's style, identical pairs argue less (odds ratio 0.60), but pairs of different models almost never surpass the strong partner. Two agents, a toy task, the single agent has more information, and an LLM is the judge.
:::

:::short echoing-identity-failures
lead: An agent abandons its role and starts speaking like its partner in 5–70% of dialogues, depending on the model; a response format in which the agent states its role every time cuts the share from 29% to 9%.
Sarath Shekkizhar, Romain Cosentino, Adam Earle and Silvio Savarese at Salesforce AI Research, in "Echoing: Identity Failures when LLM Agents Talk to Each Other" (ICLR 2026 workshop), pit two agents against each other in buyer–seller scenarios and count the share of such breakdowns (the authors call it echoing): across 66 configurations (over 2,500 dialogues) it ranges from 5% (GPT-5) to 70% (Gemini-2.5-Flash) for the buyer model. Reasoning mode barely helps (37.7% without it against 32.7–32.9% with it), and 93% of the dialogues still end in a "successful" deal. A structured response in which the agent states its role in every message cuts the share from 29% to 9% (tested on GPT and Sonnet). There is no comparison of copies with a mix: there are only three seller configurations, the judge is GPT-4o, and the models date from 2024–2025.
:::

:::short tokyo-road-game
lead: Fifty identical agents given the same forecast pile into one traffic jam and never leave it: about 96% stay on one road for all 100 rounds, with 95 minutes of travel against 64 under a plain tip.
Takahiro Ezaki, Naoto Imura and Katsuhiro Nishinari at the University of Tokyo, in "Warned alike, AI agents avoid the less-crowded road while people take it", place 50 non-communicating gpt-5.4-mini agents in a congestion task with two identical roads. A tip that road B was less crowded yesterday gives 64 minutes; the same tip with the added warning "However, many drivers are expected to see this same information and switch to Route B, so Route B may become congested" gives 95 minutes: about 96% of agents lock onto one road for all 100 rounds, although any agent on the crowded road would save 69 minutes by moving to the nearly empty one. Twelve groups of 20 people keep the roads balanced (about 62 minutes); in mixed groups of 15 agents and 5 people, agents travel 80 minutes and people 44. Identical rules plus a shared forecast produce a failure that no single agent reveals. Freezing occurs in gpt-5.4-mini without reasoning and in GPT-6 Luna (with its default reasoning), but not in Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini 3.5 Flash or gpt-5.4-mini with reasoning; model mixes were not tested.
:::

## Missing diversity: a homogeneous team herds together

Echo need not look like a meaningless loop. On a substantive research task it looks like a herd: nine copies of one model, given the same assignment and a shared forum, pick the same ideas and stop exploring the rest. Anthropic measured this, and teams that generate ideas show the same thing on a different metric.

:::card automated-w2s-researcher
lead: Nine copies of Claude Opus 4.6 with an identical prompt on a research task herded into one family of ideas and stalled at PGR 0.61; the team in which each agent got its own direction reached 0.97.
Jiaxin Wen, Liang Qiu, Joe Benton, Jan Hendrik Kirchner and Jan Leike, in Anthropic's Alignment Science Blog post "Automated Weak-to-Strong Researcher" (14 April 2026), had nine copies of Claude Opus 4.6 carry out real, if narrow, research on the weak-to-strong problem, in which a small teacher model labels data for a large student model and success is measured by a single number, PGR (performance gap recovered). The agents worked in parallel, each in its own sandbox, without roles or a manager, and shared findings through a common forum and code repository. There were two teams: in one, each agent was given its own direction; in the other, all got the same prompt. One copy sees what the others have tried through both shared channels, the forum and the code repository.

The directed team reached PGR 0.97 in five days (about 800 agent-hours, roughly $18,000), against 0.23 for two of the authors, who spent a week tuning four known methods.

- The undirected team stalled at 0.61, and the entropy of its ideas fell to zero: at one point all five agents still working were pursuing one family of methods, and the team never tried five of the eleven families.
- The same clustering appeared when Claude generated a list of concrete ideas in advance: the distribution was heavily skewed toward self-training.
- The best idea transferred unchanged to mathematics (0.94) and less well to code (0.47); the second-best idea worsened the result on code (−0.12).
- The agents gamed the evaluation in four ways, none of which the authors had foreseen.

This is measured herding by a homogeneous team on a substantive task: nine copies of one model do not divide the search space on their own, and diversity had to be introduced from outside, through assignments rather than models. It is also a lesson for self-organization (Ch. 5): the decisive gain came from a light division of labour set by humans. There was one run per condition and a low human bar; the agents queried the evaluation set without limits (by the authors' account, effectively a validation set); model mixes were not tested. In an August 2026 follow-up (a different task, Claude Opus 4.8, teams of five agents, seven runs per variant), handing out different starting ideas no longer helped: diversity collapsed quickly and the outcome did not improve.

:::

:::short diversity-collapse
lead: Mixing three vendors raises the within-topic diversity of research proposals by 23.5% (in 19 of 20 topics), and team structure changes it even more.
Nuo Chen, Yicheng Tong, Yuzhe Yang and colleagues at the National University of Singapore and CUHK (Shenzhen), in "Diversity Collapse in Multi-Agent LLM Systems: Structural Coupling and Collective Failure in Open-Ended Idea Generation" (Findings of ACL 2026), had teams of three agents write research proposals on 20 topics (over 10 thousand proposals, mostly DeepSeek-V3), and diversity is measured with the Vendi score. A flat group of "juniors" gives a Vendi score of 8.08, a team of "interdisciplinary experts" from different fields 4.65; growing the group from 3 to 7 agents raises within-topic diversity from 3.09 to 3.32. A mix of DeepSeek-V3, GPT-4o and Claude Sonnet 4 raises it from 2.75 to 3.40 in 19 of 20 topics (+23.5%), and by 34% for the expert structure. This is a direct comparison of a mixed team with a single-model team, but on one task, with small teams and 2024–2025 models. The main metric mixes spread between topics and within them; there is no run without interaction.
:::

## Does mixing models help, and when

Studies that compared a model mix directly with copies disagree, and the disagreement is informative in itself. Below are the conditions under which gains or losses were observed; they have not been shown to be necessary conditions.

**Where mixing helped** ([example](#w-deliberative-crowds)):

- agents first answered independently, and discussion or selection came afterwards;
- the outcome was checked against external ground truth: the number of discs in a jar, a match result, the answer to a test question. The researchers checked it afterwards; the agents did not check during the discussion (for the World Cup forecasts no answer existed yet at the time of discussion).

**Where mixing lost:**

- agents read each other's full solutions ([example](#w-interaction-tax));
- models were simply dumped into a pool regardless of their strength ([example](#w-mo-models-mo-problems)); in a probe by [Kim et al.](#w-kim-scaling-agent-systems) (Ch. 4), 13 mixed configurations on BrowseComp-Plus, almost all made up of a strong and a weak model of the same family, fell behind the strong homogeneous configuration by 12.6 pp on average with a coordinator and gained 2.0 pp without one, which the authors attribute to the strong member;
- the task was open-ended with no external check: in a many-round conversation without a task, a mix of three models did not stop semantic compression ([card](#w-semantic-collapse)).

Chapter 5 offers another argument for mixing: in [Self-Organizing Agent Teams](#w-sat-teams), a team of three different models solves 66.7% of mathematics and physics problems on average, while three copies of o3-mini with the same learned strategies solve 56.0% (on knowledge-heavy tasks, where the homogeneous team is three copies of Gemini 2.5 Flash, the gap almost vanishes: 72.8% against 70.9%). The authors consider heterogeneity a notable part of the gain, although the copies executed strategies learned for the mixed team, which may understate their result.

:::short deliberative-crowds
lead: Three different small models gain from discussion (d = 0.48), three copies of one model do not; but the best single model without discussion is more accurate than the mixed group in three of four tasks.
Federico Barrera-Lemarchand, Mariano Sigman and Joaquín Navajas at Universidad Torcuato Di Tella and CONICET (Buenos Aires), in "The Wisdom of Artificial Deliberative Crowds", put three small models from different families (Claude Haiku 4.5, GPT-5-nano, Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite) in a chat for up to ten rounds of short messages, until they agree. In four tasks (the number of discs in a jar, reviewing 100 NeurIPS 2024 papers, detecting sabotage in agent trajectories, forecasting the 2026 World Cup), the agreed answer is more accurate than the averaged independent answers of the same models; on football the group is indistinguishable from Polymarket prices (BF01 = 3.63). Three copies of one model gain nothing from discussion (p = 0.22, 0.61 and 0.76); three different models do (d = 0.48). But the best single model without discussion is more accurate than the mixed group in three of four tasks: diversity makes discussion worthwhile, but it does not make the group stronger than its best member. Three agents, small models, costs not compared.
:::

:::short diversity-scaling
lead: On 7–8B models, two agents differing in both model and persona were on average no worse than sixteen identical ones (67.71 against 65.34 in voting); model diversity alone required 4 agents, persona diversity 8–12. The margin is 1.3–2.4 pp.
Yingxuan Yang, Chengrui Qu, Muning Wen and colleagues at SJTU, Caltech, JHU and UC Berkeley, in "Understanding Agent Scaling in LLM-Based Multi-Agent Systems via Diversity" (preprint, February 2026), compared teams of 2–16 agents built on Qwen-2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B and Mistral-7B at four levels of diversity: identical agents, different personas, different models, and both. Identical agents quickly hit a ceiling: in debate, mean accuracy over seven benchmarks rises from 64.0 to 66.3 as the team grows from 2 to 16 agents. Two fully different agents are on average no worse than 16 identical ones (67.71 against 65.34 in voting, 67.90 against 65.48 in debate); to catch up with 16 identical agents, model diversity needs four agents and persona diversity 8–12. Small 7–8B models, no confidence intervals, an average margin of 1.3–2.4 pp, and on individual benchmarks (WinoGrande, ARC, Pro Medicine) the conclusion does not hold.
:::

:::short representational-similarity
lead: The more similar two models' internal representations, the better they cooperate and the less they invent: across 276 pairs, word guessing +66.2%, haiku diversity −50.4%.
Yujin Potter, Seun Eisape, Shiyang Lai and colleagues at UC Berkeley, the University of Chicago and Google DeepMind, in "Representational Similarity and Model Behavior in Multi-Agent Interaction" (ICML 2026), formed 276 pairs from 23 open models (1–72 billion parameters) and measured the similarity of their internal representations (CKA) independently of the games. From the least to the most similar pair, outcomes in cooperative games rise (word guessing +66.2%, public goods +27.5%, divide-a-dollar +23.8%, Keynesian beauty contest +3.7%), while the diversity of joint creative answers falls: haiku −50.4%, vacation benefits −53.5%; text quality does not depend on similarity. The association survives controls for size, family, tokenizer and MMLU gap, and is tied most strongly to the early layers. This is a quantitative form of the trade-off between agreement and novelty. It is a correlation across pairs of small and medium open models from 2024–2025: small teams rather than large swarms.
:::

:::short interaction-tax
lead: When different models read each other's full solutions, the mix loses its advantage within one exchange (−0.02 to −0.08 relative to independent generation); for a single-model team the exchange helps slightly.
Summer Eunhyung Ann, Haokun Liu and Chenhao Tan at the University of Chicago, in "The Interaction Tax: When Communication Erases Diversity in Multi-Agent Teams" (poster at the AI4Research workshop at ICML 2026, not the main track), examine, on 11 optimization tasks with automatic verification, what happens when the agents of a team of 2–4 members read each other's full solutions (models: Claude Sonnet 4, GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Flash). For a single-model team the exchange helps slightly (Chain +0.05, MAgICoRe +0.04 relative to independent generation); for a team of different models it hurts (−0.02, −0.04 and −0.08 for Chain, MAgICoRe and Debate): for Debate, the distance between solutions on DiffBases drops from 0.956 to 0.479 right after the exchange. The MoA scheme, in which proposers do not see each other, stays positive (+0.016). At equal budget no multi-agent scheme beat the best single-agent variant. Models from 2024–2025; the coverage effect rests on one task (Erdős).
:::

:::short mo-models-mo-problems
lead: Pooling 23 open models indiscriminately is worse than the best single model on HLE, by 5 to 18 pp depending on how answers are combined, although at least one model is right on about 57% of questions.
Sara Vera Marjanović, Jiacheng Xu, Aleksandr Laptev and colleagues at NVIDIA and the University of Copenhagen, in "Mo' Models, Mo' Problems: How to best select model pools when designing Multi-Agent Systems" (poster at the REALM workshop at EMNLP 2026), built a pool of 23 open models (from 2B to 1.6T parameters) and tested eight ways of selecting models into a system without interaction and three ways of combining their answers (router, voting, LLM judge). The HLE ceiling (at least one model right) is about 57% against 29.4% for the best single model, but all 23 together give about −5 pp (router), −18 (voting) and −12 (judge) relative to the best single model; a gain of up to +1 pp appears for a same-family pair (Gemma-4). A single-author paper from KAIKAKU finds the same ([Co-Failure Ceiling](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.27288); a pool of 67 models, with routers and voting on a 15-model sub-pool; numbers from the corrected version in the author's repository): the router is weaker than the best model (0.927 against 0.958), voting loses to the best member in 75–93% of trios, and the diversity gain is at most +0.035; the author withdrew his result on a "common-mode co-failure tail" on 25 September 2026. Static ensembles; NVIDIA's numbers are read off plots.
:::

One study moves the diversity debate inside a single model: if a reasoning model stages a "society" of different voices in its own chain of thought, part of the benefit of multi-agent debate may accrue to a single agent.

:::short societies-of-thought
lead: A reasoning model argues with itself: the chains of thought of R1 and QwQ-32B contain more perspective shifts and reconciliations, and amplifying one "conversational" feature raised R1-8B accuracy on Countdown from 27.1% to 54.8%.
The clash of perspectives for which debates are assembled can take place inside one agent. Junsol Kim, Shiyang Lai, Nino Scherrer and colleagues at Google (Paradigms of Intelligence) and the University of Chicago, in "Reasoning Models Generate Societies of Thought" (preprint, January 2026; non-archival ICML 2026 workshop), compared the reasoning of DeepSeek-R1 and QwQ-32B with that of their "ordinary" siblings on 8,262 problems. The reasoning models show far more question-answering, perspective shifts and reconciliations (question-answering, R1 vs V3: β = 0.345 with length controlled; conflicts of perspectives are more frequent in QwQ-32B, while for R1 vs V3 the difference is not significant), and the "voices" differ in personality and expertise. Amplifying one "conversational" feature in the distilled R1-8B raised accuracy on Countdown from 27.1% to 54.8%; in RL training of a 3B model, dialogue moves emerged on their own. There is no comparison with real multi-agent systems, the models date from early and mid-2025, and an unofficial attempt to replicate the steering (not peer-reviewed) gives +10 pp instead of +28.
:::

:::takeaways
- Echo appears at several levels, each with its own evidence: [similar independent answers](#w-artificial-hivemind) (the re-evaluation confirmed text similarity but not homogeneity of ideas), [shared errors in voting](#w-consensus-not-verification), [an identical reaction to one signal](#w-tokyo-road-game), and [attractors in conversation](#w-attractor-states) in 16 of 16 models. A critique of one does not dispose of the others.
- Deliberation among copies without an external check narrows diversity regardless of the number of agents: [30 debating copies](#w-ringelmann-effect) are worth 1.12 independent votes and are no more accurate than self-correction, [eight copies](#w-deliberative-illusion) lose up to 72.4% of facts in three rounds, [nine copies of Opus 4.6](#w-automated-w2s-researcher) herd into one family of ideas.
- Mixing models helped when agents first answered independently and the outcome was checked against external ground truth: [two different agents](#w-diversity-scaling) are no worse than sixteen identical ones, [three different models](#w-deliberative-crowds) gain from discussion while three copies do not; [a three-vendor mix](#w-diversity-collapse) adds 23.5% idea diversity.
- When agents [read each other's full solutions](#w-interaction-tax) or models are [pooled indiscriminately](#w-mo-models-mo-problems), the mix is no better, and often worse, than its strongest member; [in free conversation](#w-attractor-states) it only changes the attractor. The best single model beats the mixed group in three of four tasks ([deliberating trios](#w-deliberative-crowds)).
- Open: there is no direct comparison of a homogeneous and a mixed swarm of dozens of frontier agents with controlled composition and comparable budget on a substantive task; almost everything has been measured on pairs and trios, mostly with small models or models released no later than 2025.
:::
