How does a group of agents arrive at a shared belief? Why do copies of one model converge on one answer, what decides whether it is right, and what happens inside a model reading another agent's message? The chapter draws on four lines of evidence. A toy swarm with a known correct answer allows targeted interventions and lets researchers trace how a change spreads through the group. Statistical-physics models link conformity, the number of interlocutors and sampling randomness. Majority and convention maps show when a population of copies settles on a shared norm and what it takes to overturn it. Probes read inside agents what never appears in their messages. The last section audits how far simulations of "societies" can be trusted.

:::callout Chapter glossary: temperature, conformity, phase transition
**Temperature** is the degree of randomness in a model's answers: at zero, copies answer almost identically; at high temperature, their answers scatter. **Conformity (λ)** is how strongly an agent adjusts to answers already given. This is not Toby Ord's λ from Ch. 4, where λ measures how well work divides among the agents of a swarm. **Majority strength (β)** is the same quantity in models without an own bias. **Own bias (h)** is where the model leans when the opinions around it are split evenly. A **phase transition** is an abrupt change of regime: while conformity times the number of interlocutors is smaller than the noise, the group's answers stay mixed; once it outweighs the noise, the group locks onto one answer. In finite groups the transition is smoothed out, so the authors speak of a "crossover". A **probe** is a simple model that reads an agent's internal activations. **Patching** swaps one agent's evidence or activations for ones taken from elsewhere, to test what exactly influenced the group's answer.
:::

## The Flag Game: a toy swarm with a known answer

Analysing a swarm with the methods of neural-network interpretability (probes, patching, searching for the "circuits" through which one agent's belief changes another's answer) requires an experiment with a known ground truth and the ability to intervene precisely. The Flag Game authors put this approach in their title as "mechanistic swarm interpretability". The work is explicitly motivated by the July OpenAI and Hugging Face incident (Ch. 3), in which a false belief about the scorer spread through a message board the agents had set up for themselves.

:::card flag-game
lead: Toy swarm with a known answer: a GPT-4o team's accuracy peaks at ≈0.63 with 16 agents and rises no further; in larger groups, wrong consensus gives way to a split into two camps.
**What they did.** Elizabeth Pavlova and Hidenori Tanaka (NTT Research, Harvard; preprint, 16 September 2026) built a toy swarm with a known correct answer. Each agent sees only a crop of the flag of one of 28 countries and exchanges guesses with the others: in pairs, through a shared board, or through a crop-blind manager. They varied the number of agents (4–128), the wording on how much to trust peers, team composition (GPT-4o and GPT-5.4) and the communication protocol; separately, they swapped one agent's crop and tracked how the change spread through the group.
**Results.**
- More agents is not monotonically better: accuracy is ≈0.54 with 4 agents, peaks at ≈0.63 with 16, then ≈0.59 at 64–128 (all GPT-4o, pairwise exchange, 40 seeds per point, ≈38 valid).
- The type of error changes: "wrong consensus" occurs in about 26% of runs with 4 agents and 0% with 32 and 128; a "split into two camps" in about 24% with 4 and about 60% with 128.
- A single prompt line on trusting peers raises accuracy on the shared board from 0.54 to 0.81; under pairwise exchange the optimum is α = 0.75.
- Mixed GPT-4o + GPT-5.4 teams reach ≈0.70 versus ≈0.55 for an all-GPT-4o team and ≈0.64 for an all-GPT-5.4 team: the models make different errors (in 100 probes, GPT-5.4 named a country incompatible with its crop 18 times, GPT-4o 5 times).
- Patching the crop of the most influential agent in one game raised group accuracy from 25% to 100%; patching the same fraction of agents (1/8) gives on average +40% with 8 agents and about +17% with 128.

**Why it matters.** The work carries the core interpretability technique, an experiment with a known ground truth and targeted intervention, over to swarms and provides a vocabulary for analysing the incident: in the authors' view, the greater danger is not a split but unanimity under group pressure. For swarm design, it implies that team composition and communication structure change the outcome at least as much as the choice of model, although in this experiment the communication structure is imposed from outside.
**Caveats.** A toy task, and the authors warn that the conclusions should not be transferred to deployed swarms. Group size was varied only for GPT-4o, and GPT-5.4 was tested only with 8 agents; there are no significance tests (the peak of 0.63 versus 0.59 is small relative to the shaded band in the plot). The 25% → 100% effect comes from one selected game; on the shared board, the outcome of the discussion roughly equals a simple majority vote over the initial guesses.

## Temperature, conformity and phase transition

In multi-agent debates, agents answer independently, then see the others' answers and revise their own. Three works describe these dynamics with quantitative models: Maya Okawa (NTT Research, Harvard) with a theory of conformity and temperature, a Stanford group with an Ising-type model fitted on thousands of communities, and a Göttingen group with a decomposition of opinion shifts in pairs into the partner's influence and the model's pull toward its own position. A fourth work (Ricco et al.) studies the same dynamics in populations of up to 1,000 copies on lattices and separately measures factual correctness.

:::card okawa-biased-consensus
lead: In debates among copies, a weak model bias becomes a near-unanimous decision when conformity times the number of interlocutors outweighs the sampling temperature (λρN/T ≳ 1); experiments use groups of 3–11 agents and pre-2026 models.
**What they did.** Maya Okawa (NTT Research, Harvard; ICML 2026 poster) tested what happens to a weak bias of a single model in multi-agent debates. Realistic tasks: ten GPT-4.1 Nano "financial advisers" build a portfolio (the bias is the share of US assets), and six GPT-3.5 or GPT-4 "judges" pick the better answer on 148 MT-Bench questions (the bias is a preference for "their own" answers). Synthetic tasks: a choice between neutral symbols or between Jane and John, where any agreement is already a bias. The main control knob is sampling temperature.
**Results.**
- Low temperature turns a weak bias into unanimity: at temperatures up to ≈0.6 the US share of the portfolio is ≈0.97, at 1.1 and above ≈0.85; market-adjusted return is worse at low temperature (≈1.05 versus ≈1.37). For GPT-3.5 judges, self-preference is ≈1.0 at T = 0 and ≈0.80 at 1.2 and above, and agreement with human labels is ≈0.855 at low temperature and ≈0.89 at high temperature.
- Theory: a collective norm emerges when conformity λ times the number of interlocutors ρN outweighs the temperature (λρN/T ≳ 1); in finite groups the transition is smoothed out and becomes sharper as N grows.
- Phase diagrams for GPT-4.1 Nano and Llama 3.1 405B follow the prediction: for Llama, consensus is ≈0.98 at T ≤ 0.3 and ≈0.38 at 1.5.
- From debate transcripts, each of 11 models received a "profile" of conformity and bias: DeepSeek V3 conforms most strongly, while the GPT-4.1 family has negative conformity.
- Mixing models in triads and mixing temperatures weakens the bias, but only modestly in the realistic tasks (temperature mix in the portfolio task: bias 0.918 → 0.898).

**Why it matters.** Here the echo is derived rather than observed: identical copies at the same low temperature react identically and inflate a shared bias, while different models or temperatures average out the reactions. This yields levers (randomness, sparser links, hiding neighbours' confidence, mixing models) and a warning for densely connected swarms: the more interlocutors, the lower the threshold.
**Caveats.** Pre-2026 models (no Claude, Gemini or GPT-5-class models), groups of 3–11 agents; the phase transition is shown rigorously only in theory. GPT-4.1 Nano has negative conformity, so its unanimity rests on the identical bias of its copies, not on peer pressure.

:::short physics-of-agents
lead: Groups of 32 copies move from indifference to consensus or polarization within eight rounds, and an Ising-type model predicts an agent's next opinion with 75–86 balanced accuracy.
Stanford (Ganguli, Zou; preprint, August 2026): about 10,000 communities of 32 agents, 8 rounds of short messages over a graph, MATH problems and political statements, models GPT-4o-mini, Gemma-3n-E4B, Qwen3.5-9B and Llama-3.1-8B. An Ising-type model with three coupling coefficients predicts an agent's next opinion with 75–86 balanced accuracy. The fitted parameters place the groups below the critical "social temperature": conviction grows in every condition, and groups move from indifference to consensus or polarization. On mathematics, a wrong majority becomes right more often than the reverse (GPT-4o-mini: 28% versus 11% of groups), while on politics three models out of four drift to the right (for Gemma, the share of groups with a right-leaning majority rises from 75% to 96%). The echo takes hold even when agents have different personas. Caveats: votes are answers without reasoning, each group uses a single model, and GPT-5.6-sol and DeepSeek-V4-Flash were tested only as a check of the predictor.
:::

:::short brockers-opinion-dynamics
lead: The "agreement" of two copies is largely the model's prior opinion rather than the outcome of an exchange of arguments.
Viola Priesemann's group (Max Planck Institute for Dynamics and Self-Organization, Göttingen; Nature Communications, 22 September 2026) had two agents of the same model debate for five rounds on 12 topics and used a Bayesian model to split opinion shifts into the partner's influence and three biases: toward the model's own position on the topic, toward agreement, and toward the first statement. For five of six models (Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, two Mixtral variants, GPT-4o-mini), opinions converge within one or two rounds to a topic-dependent attractor; the sixth, Grok-4.1-fast, shows no convergence. Topic bias has the greatest standalone explanatory power for all six models. In GPT-4o-mini and Grok, however, the interaction effect tends to exceed the topic-bias effect in strength. The partner’s influence decays within a few rounds. Fine-tuning on climate messages shifts the attractor (Mixtral). Limitations: pairs only, small models without reasoning, 12 topics.
:::

:::short rheon-consensus
lead: In populations of up to 1,000 copies of one model, answers quickly converge in meaning, but consensus does not guarantee factual correctness: on average the exchange repaired answers more often than it damaged them, yet in 85 of 1,800 runs the hallucination share rose markedly.
Ricco, Onofri, Sammartino and Di Pietro (KAUST; Sammartino also University of Pisa; preprint, 30 September 2026) place copies of one frozen Qwen3-14B model (4-bit) on lattices of different geometry: a ring of 8, 16 and 32 agents (2 neighbours), a 2D torus of 64, 144 and 256 (4 neighbours), a 3D torus of 64, 216, 512 and 1,000 (6 neighbours) and an all-to-all graph of 32 and 64. Agents first answer independently, then rewrite their answers in random sequential order, reading their neighbours and their own previous answer: 10 sweeps, up to 100 new tokens per response. 6 FreshQA questions × 12 size/geometry settings × 6 temperatures give 432 configurations with 5 trials each, 2,160 runs in total; the 4,767,840 model calls count responses, not population size.
- **Meaning and facts diverge.** Semantic cohesion increases almost everywhere. On five questions the exchange repaired answers more often than it damaged them on average, but in 85 of 1,800 runs the hallucination fraction rose by more than 0.1 — this is not a benchmark quality score.
- **Judge validation.** Human validation showed that on the false-premise question the judge systematically inverted its verdicts; the authors excluded that question from the pooled factual analysis while keeping the data.
- **Temperature and timing.** The optimal temperature depends on what the authors call model coupling, and most of the shift happens in the first sweeps; these are the authors' conclusions within 10 sweeps, not general operating rules.

Caveats: one model; the Qwen2.5-72B judge sees the question and the answer but not the gold answer; in the broad comparison population size is partly confounded with geometry (narrower comparisons at equal N = 64); the ≈$540 figure is a hypothetical cloud-tariff estimate, not a bill (the campaign ran about 15 days on two RTX A6000 GPUs); licence CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. Added 9 October 2026: the preprint appeared before the cut-off but was not in the October corpus.
:::

## Majority strength: maps of conventions and tipping points

Another branch of the same physics looks not at debates but at pure coordination: a population of copies chooses one of two words while seeing the others' choices. The quantities measured are majority strength β, the critical group size above which consensus is no longer reached, and the share of a stubborn minority that can overturn an established norm. Two of these works appeared in Science Advances and PNAS in 2026 and two are 2026 preprints, but all experiments use models from 2023–2025. The last card in this section (Banerjee and Moghaddas) is not a word game but the spread of a belief across networks of 30 agents on two small open models.

:::short prev-demarzo-majority
lead: The more capable the model, the more strongly its copies follow the majority; the critical group size up to which copies reach consensus is about 30 for Llama 3 70B and about 1,000 for GPT-4 Turbo.
De Marzo, Castellano and Garcia (Konstanz, Rome; Science Advances, 14 August 2026) measured how a population of copies of one model chooses between the meaningless labels k and z while seeing the others' answers. The probability of joining the majority falls on a single curve P(m) = ½[tanh(βm) + 1] with majority strength β (the authors' "majority force"). Critical group size: about 30 for Llama 3 70B, about 80 for GPT-4o, about 1,000 for GPT-4 Turbo; for Claude 3.5 Sonnet, majority strength at N = 1,000 is still above the threshold, so this is a lower bound. The correlation of MMLU with majority strength β is 0.79 (p = 0.006), and with log N_c 0.82 (p = 0.013); for N_c, the authors themselves say the relation with most other benchmarks is not significant. This is the echo of copies in pure form, but on snapshots of 2023–2024 models, with no correct answer and no model mixing; the N = 1,000 point for GPT-4 Turbo is a single run.
:::

:::short prev-conformity-misalignment
lead: A majority of copies can get stuck against the model's own bias: more than 60% of opinion pairs do so for Gemma 3 27B, and the share depends strongly on the model.
The same authors, joined by Bellina and Priesemann (preprint, 11 May 2026), added an own bias h to the formula (the model's leaning toward one of two meaningful slogans) and searched for this trap (β > 1) with 50 copies and 100 opinion pairs across nine models: for Gemma 3 27B it holds more than 60% of pairs, for gemini-2.5-flash-lite 60%, for gpt-5-mini 30%. Adding 35 stubborn agents to 50 regular ones flips the pair on gender self-identification so that the population stays on the new side until the end of observation even after they leave, while 225 fail to lock in the pair on renewable energy. Agreement among copies is not the same as the model's preference on its own. Caveats: h is a pull toward a slogan, not refusal of a harmful request; reasoning is off or minimal; the social signal is written into the prompt as a list.
:::

:::short prev-flint-group-size
lead: As a population of copies grows, the outcome becomes deterministic: for GPT on the White/African pair, the share of the strong word rises from 0.599 at N = 3 to 1.00 at N = 1,000.
Flint, Aiello, Pastor-Satorras and Baronchelli (London, Copenhagen, Barcelona; PNAS, August 2026) study homogeneous populations of Phi-4, GPT-4o, QwQ-32B and Llama 3.1 70B in a naming game: a random pair chooses one of two socially loaded words. At N = 24, interaction amplifies an individual bias, creates one from a neutral start, or reverses it, and the direction depends on the model: on the her/his pair, Qwen and Phi converge on her, GPT and Llama on his, from almost the same individual tendency. As N grows, the outcome becomes deterministic (the White/African pair for GPT: 0.599 at N = 3, 1.00 at N = 1,000). Large populations are computed from pre-extracted policies, the models date from 2024–2025, and the authors state explicitly that this is not misalignment with human values.
:::

:::short indirect-tipping
lead: The cost of overturning a convention depends on direction: on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, a 3% committed minority flips "sadness → joy", while the reverse requires more than 63%.
Baronchelli's group (preprint, 21 September 2026) computes what share of stubborn agents, who always name the same word, overrides an established convention. On DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, the transition "sadness → joy" takes 3% of the population, the reverse requires more than 63%, and "anticipation → trust" cannot be forced at any share. An established convention is sometimes cheaper to break by a detour through a third word than head-on, although fewer than 10% of paths are like that, and staying too long on the intermediate word locks it in. The maps are built for four small open models (Qwen2.5-7B, Phi-4, Llama-3.2-3B and this distilled model) from the cached next-token distribution. A population's equilibrium can be broken by choosing a route, not only by a direct attack on a single agent. An important caveat: Barrie and Törnberg read the same prompt as leakage from training data (see the table), and each model has its own map.
:::

:::short coherence-driven-contagion
lead: Belief spreads through a population of LLM agents as a complex contagion: at equal degree, a clustered network ends with a higher believing fraction than a rewired one (0.67 vs 0.43) — in populations of 30 agents on two small models.
Tathagata Banerjee (Takeda Pharmaceuticals) and Nima Moghaddas (Northeastern University; preprint, 2 October 2026, accepted at the NeurIPS 2026 FAST workshop, not the main conference) state in the prompt how many of 10 peers endorsed a claim and measure adoption from the first-token logits of ADOPT/DECLINE, not from free-form discussion; claim plausibility, source reliability and persona are varied. Populations of 30 agents on Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3; the claims are temperature and commute-time predictions.
- **Clustering amplifies contagion.** At matched degree 6 in the marginal regime, a clustered Watts–Strogatz ring ends with a believing fraction of 0.67 vs 0.43 in the rewired network (one-sided Mann–Whitney p = 2.7×10⁻⁶, rank-biserial effect 0.37; Qwen, temperature predictions; 100 replicates of 15 steps, seed fraction 0.12, condition T = 36 °C).
- **Hysteresis.** On a Barabási–Albert graph (m = 3) the authors find a bifurcation and hysteresis: under pressure from permanent believers, with an independently measured retention kernel, consensus can persist even after these believers are removed.
- **Threshold.** The first principal component captures 98% of the reduction in the adoption threshold, and an additive model 92% (R²) — a behavioural description, not proof of an internal representation in the model.

Authors' caveats: small populations, stylized networks, two models and two domains, one isolated belief, unverifiable predictions rather than facts. In addition, updates are driven by a logit-based kernel, not by free-form agent interaction. Added 9 October 2026: the preprint appeared before the cut-off but was not in the October corpus.
:::

## Probes inside agents

Physical models work with answers. Interpretability goes deeper: it reads the receiving model's activations, patches the internal trace of a message and trains probes that see in the group what is not visible in the text. Two works on open-weight models show that an echo chamber and collusion among copies leave a distinguishable internal trace. The work placed between them does not look inside: it counts from answers alone how often a homogeneous triad loses a correct majority, and its sample includes closed frontier models.

:::short social-circuits
lead: Another agent's message leaves a distinguishable internal trace in the receiving model: on 2Wiki, restoring activations reproduces the answer change in 96.73–97.81% of cases, on a synthetic task with a shared error in 1–16%.
Researchers from Simon Fraser University, the University of Alberta and SUSTech (preprint, 28 September 2026) examine from the inside how one agent's message changes another's answer: the receiver is run with and without the text, and then, on a corrupted text, a slice of the activations recorded under the original message is restored. This reproduces the answer change on 2Wiki in 97.81% (Gemma-3-4B) and 96.73% (Qwen3-4B) of cases, and on a synthetic task with a shared error in 1–16%. For Qwen3-4B, 5.70% of messages correct an answer and 1.20% spoil a correct one. More copies of a message bring both more corrections and more contagion: with seven messages, a paraphrase flips 73.19% of correct answers and "different evidence" 95.14%. Selecting messages by activations (CGD) is no worse than multi-agent schemes and cheaper; ordinary debate with Gemma on 2Wiki, with the token budget cut to that of CGD, drops to 7.22% versus 24.72% for a single agent that receives only its assigned passages; the available information is not matched. White-box access is required; the models have 4–8B parameters.
:::

:::short homogeneous-panel-collapse
lead: How often a triad of copies loses a correct majority in debate depends on the model: from 43.90% for Qwen3.5-9B to zero for Gemma-4-31B and DeepSeek V4 Flash (14 models, MMLU-Pro questions).
Nanyang Technological University (NeurIPS 2026; Evaluations and Datasets Track, according to the authors) has three copies of one model debate MMLU-Pro questions and keeps separate counts of two outcomes: loss of a correct majority and correction of a wrong one. Loss rates in the 14-model table: 43.90% for Qwen3.5-9B, 10.24% for Phi-4-mini, 2.15% for Claude Sonnet 4.5, 0.70% for GPT-5.4-mini, 0.59% for Gemini 3 Flash, zero for Gemma-4-31B and DeepSeek V4 Flash. In 6,925 debates of six models, a correct majority turned wrong 253 times, and 58.9% of collapses begin in the first round. The same triads also repair wrong answers, so freezing the debate when behavioural probes signal a risk prevents 29 collapses but loses 108 corrections (net −79), and private self-revision is 1.44–4.81 points worse than debate if ties count as errors (otherwise nearly level). Mixed panels are only a pilot on 200 questions, and for closed models the result is not always small: Claude Haiku 4.5 loses 11.19% of correct majorities in an early cross-check sample.
:::

:::short narcbench-helios
lead: A linear deception probe plus a group statistic detects instructed collusion (best method: AUROC 0.99–1.00 for all four models), while unprompted collusion is caught poorly (AUROC 0.49–0.73).
Oxford, ELLIS Tübingen and UCL (version 3, October 2026) describe the HELIOS framework and the NARCBench benchmark: four copies of one open-weight model, two of which are asked to collude secretly. On steganographic blackjack, the best method reaches AUROC 0.99–1.00 for all four models, while transcript judges without a description of the scheme are helpless: Qwen3-32B detects collusion in 1 game out of 20, and Gemma-4 31B and Claude Sonnet 5 without a hint flag all 20 collusion games and 19–20 of 20 control games. Where nobody instructed collusion, it is verbalized in the text in 2 episodes out of 229, and probe transfer gives AUROC 0.49–0.73. The copies agree on a code in ordinary speech; no comparison with a mixed-model table was made.
:::

## How far simulations of societies can be trusted

Almost all of this literature rests on "societies" of copies of a single model with a prompt describing a social experiment. Two 2026 works check how much of the observed effects comes from dynamics and how much from experimental design; the third measures emergence with information-theoretic measures and tests significance at the level of individual groups.

:::short pimmur-principles
lead: An audit of 576 simulations of "societies": 91.1% violate at least one of six PIMMUR principles, models recognize the intended experiment in 65.2% of cases, and in redesigned experiments some effects weaken.
Eight authors (CUHK, Johns Hopkins, CMU, Fudan, Stanford, Renmin University of China; preprint, version 4, September 2026) reviewed 576 simulations of "societies" from 350 papers against the six PIMMUR principles: diverse profiles, real interaction, memory, minimal control, unawareness of the hypothesis, and grounding in human data. At least one principle is violated by 91.1% of studies; the first three are met in 85.4%, 92.6% and 83.5% of cases, the last three in 46.7%, 37.7% and 36.1%, and these are upper bounds. Six 2026 models recognized the intended experiment in 65.2% of cases. After redesign to PIMMUR the effects weaken: agents "infected" by fake news 56% → 33% (gpt-3.5), balanced triads 61% → 34% (Llama-3-70B), and the "telephone game" is statistically indistinguishable from a reverse instruction; the power-law tail of the network survived. Part of the reported collective effects on older models is explained by the prompt and experimental design rather than by dynamics.
:::

:::short prev-robustness-audits
lead: Writing the same persona as a paragraph or as a list changes the cooperation rate in a Prisoner's Dilemma by 76 percentage points (gpt-5.2); for other models the gap ranges from ≈1 to ≈77.
Ye, Cao, Chen and Ferrara (USC; preprint, 17 May 2026) argue that no scientific claims should be drawn from LLM social simulations without robustness audits and propose the TRAILS taxonomy. Their testbed is a 10-round Prisoner's Dilemma (gpt-5.2, 30 seeds), where three renderings of the same persona produce a gap of 76 pp; for claude-haiku-4-5 it is ≈77, for gemini-2.5-flash ≈36, for deepseek-v3 ≈1. In a network of 100 agents with frozen stances, a small increase in initial homophily raises assortativity from 0.142 to 0.247–0.287 and pushes the share of within-group links past one half. The collective picture of a single-model swarm depends both on the model and on prompt formatting, which plays no part in the claimed mechanism.
:::

:::short riedl-emergent-coordination
lead: Ten copies of GPT-4.1 synchronize on a shared signal rather than forming an organizational structure; each extra member multiplies the odds of success by 0.92.
Christoph Riedl (Northeastern University; ICLR 2026 poster) measures emergence with information-theoretic measures: agents name numbers whose sum must hit a hidden target, nobody sees the others' numbers, and the only shared signal is "too high" or "too low". In a grid of 7,150 groups, each extra member multiplies the odds of success by 0.92. In the main experiment (10 agents, 200 groups per condition), the three prompts (plain, personas, "think about what the others will do") do not differ in success rate. Only the last one, however, makes the group's future predictable from triplets (p = 3.5×10⁻¹⁴), although the gain of a triplet over the best pair is about zero: the alignment is redundant, not a "role beyond the pair". Only 3.5% of groups are individually significant; differences between copies are stable but do not add up to an organizational structure. The model is the 2025 GPT-4.1, there is no communication channel, and the author himself does not compare the team with a single agent.
:::

:::takeaways
- Unanimity among copies emerges when conformity times the number of interlocutors outweighs sampling randomness (λρN/T ≳ 1), but often rests simply on a shared bias of identical copies ([Okawa](#w-okawa-biased-consensus), [Göttingen](#w-brockers-opinion-dynamics)). Copies of more capable models follow the majority more strongly ([De Marzo et al.](#w-prev-demarzo-majority)).
- Group size changes the kind of error, not its presence: in the [Flag Game](#w-flag-game), small groups err together, large ones split, accuracy stops rising after 16 agents, patching 1/8 of agents loses force as swarms grow, and mixed GPT-4o + GPT-5.4 teams beat homogeneous ones.
- A stubborn minority can overturn an established norm, and the threshold depends on the model, the opinion pair and the route: in one direction 3% of the population suffices, in the reverse more than 63% is needed ([majority trap](#w-prev-conformity-misalignment), [tipping maps](#w-indirect-tipping)).
- Activation probes see what is not in the text: the internal trace of another agent's message ([social circuits](#w-social-circuits)) and instructed collusion ([NARCBench](#w-narcbench-helios)). So far this works only on open-weight models with access to weights, and unprompted collusion is caught poorly.
- Open question: how far the quantitative thresholds transfer to real swarms. Transfer across model generations has not been established, models recognize the intended experiment ([PIMMUR](#w-pimmur-principles)), prompt formatting shifts the result ([robustness audit](#w-prev-robustness-audits)), and mixed teams have been tested only in small groups.
