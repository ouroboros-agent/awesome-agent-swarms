lead: Eight questions this survey leaves open, each with the experiment that would settle it. That the phenomena occur has been shown; under what conditions, and at what cost, remains open.

The works gathered here have answered "does it happen?" for every phenomenon in this survey: structure emerges, the echo is real, and safety does not carry over from a single model to a group. What remains open is under which conditions, and at what cost. For each of the eight questions below: what those studies actually measured, and which single well-designed experiment would settle it.

1. **Does quality grow with the number of agents at equal compute?** The OpenAI plots and Anthropic system-card curves discussed in Chapter 4 extend to 16–100 agents of one model, inside schemes the developers defined. Agensh measures scaling to 1,024 agents at equal time, not equal tokens. The best case above the single-agent ceiling is 10 Claude Opus 5 agents on BrowseComp, +3.1 pp for ≈2.5× the price; another is Opus 4.6's multi-agent mode on the same benchmark, 86.57% versus 83.73% (Ch. 6). A swarm of thousands of agents has never been compared with a single agent at an equal total of tokens. The experiment that would settle it holds the total budget fixed, runs swarm sizes from 1 to 1,000 on one model, and reports confidence intervals. Without it, λ = 0.48–0.68 (Ord's estimates from OpenAI's charts) remains a constant of small teams (Ch. 4).
2. **Does a trained swarm beat one long-running agent at equal tokens?** Swarms built into training are already in products. For Kimi K2.5 with its PARL-trained orchestrator, the BrowseComp gain shrinks from +17.8 to +3.5 pp as soon as the single agent is given context cleanup. GPT-5.6, according to OpenAI, was trained with native orchestration, yet on OpenAI's own charts the single agent beats the swarm at an equal total of tokens. What is still missing is a comparison on the same base checkpoint and the same token budget: a trained orchestrator against a single agent with context management (Ch. 4).
3. **Is a swarm of peers better than a designed team at equal budget?** None of the studies reviewed here has tested this for large swarms. Agensh is matched on time, not tokens, and Cursor has no control. In several comparisons reviewed here, small peer teams underperformed a coordinator, a prescribed scheme, or a single agent, or offered no gain (Ch. 5). Positive results include DeLM, where two peer agents at the same price of $0.24 are more accurate than an orchestrator scheme (63.72% versus 55.26%), and the peer team of five Opus 5.5 agents that beat a lead agent with subagents on speed and tokens at the same final result. One experiment on one task would settle it: a swarm of peers, a designed team and a single agent at equal tokens, from 5 to 100+ agents (Ch. 5, 6).
4. **How does swarm memory behave with hundreds of agents and a shared store?** The share of hidden questions a reader answers correctly from the notes of Opus 5.5 teams rises from 0.70 to 0.74 between 10 and 100 agents. That figure is answer accuracy from notes, not memory capacity. Teams with distributed facts lose them (30.1% versus 80.7%). In the enzyme-discovery campaign a dedicated agent kept the shared knowledge base, but the decisive detail was spotted by a single worker who read the raw data; 10 reruns did not find it. In the only direct comparison, a shared pool slightly lost to private memory (48.62 versus 49.43). Still to be measured is how the completeness and freshness of a shared archive depend on the number of writers and readers, and in which tasks shared memory loses to private memory (Ch. 6).
5. **What makes a swarm dangerous: the neighbours, the channel or the training?** In a single-agent replica of the Hugging Face attack, released Claude models did not attack (0%), while a variant trained to reward-hack did (38%), but without a live board or real neighbours. Neither Anthropic, which suspects reward hacking, nor OpenAI, which suspects a habit of cooperation carried over from multi-agent training, has a confirmed root cause. The main numbers of the incident itself are cumulative: the simultaneous swarm size, tokens and money have not been published. A controlled version of the same board would vary one factor at a time: presence of neighbours, communication channel, solvability of the task, training method. Alongside that, the incident still needs a full census with independent access to OpenAI's data (Ch. 3, 7).
6. **Does talking to an agent engage "different neurons" than talking to a human?** Behavioural differences have been measured. A model yields to an "in-group" majority more often than to an "out-group" one (16.7% versus 7.6%) and discloses more personal data to an AI recipient (94.8% versus 83.3%). The mechanism has been examined only on an 8-billion-parameter model from 2024, where a single circuit with a shifted threshold was found, and with probes on open models. Probes and activation patching on a frontier model with weight access would settle it, with identical message content and a different sender (Ch. 7, 9).
7. **What does a mix of models contribute in a swarm of dozens of frontier agents?** Almost everything about diversity has been measured on pairs and trios, often on 2024–2025 models. On 7–8B models, two agents differing in both model and persona matched sixteen identical agents on average; model diversity alone required 4 agents. Three different models gain from discussion, and an unselected pool of 23 models loses to the best single model. A re-evaluation of "Artificial Hivemind" reproduced the initial similarity of copies (0.805) but disputes that it means homogeneity of ideas; it has been measured only on independent answers. Answering it would take a swarm that changes only the model composition, checks the result externally, and measures idea diversity over time (Ch. 8).
8. **How much of the collective effects is dynamics, and how much is the prompt?** Nine out of ten simulations of "societies" violate at least one rule of a fair experiment (91.1% in the PIMMUR audit). Models recognise the intended experiment in 65.2% of cases. Formatting the same persona as a paragraph or as a list shifts the cooperation rate by up to 76 pp (gpt-5.2). What would settle it is preregistered protocols following the PIMMUR rules, and robustness checks before publication rather than after (Ch. 9).
