:::findings
1. **In the matched-token lab curves collected here, a strong single agent matched or beat the swarm, whose measured gain was wall-clock time; quality gains above the single-agent baseline were measured in specific settings.** At equal total output tokens, OpenAI's published GPT-5.6 Sol curves favour one agent over 4- and 16-agent swarms; 5 Opus 5.5 agents reach the same ProgramBench level 2.7× faster at ≈1.9× tokens. Quality gains were reported with verifiable progress and shared findings (8.0% vs 2.2% of ARC-AGI-3 games), with coordination learned in advance (finding 2), or at a higher spend (10 Opus 5 agents: +3.1 pp on BrowseComp at ≈2.5× cost). What is held equal — tokens, dollars or time — differs between studies (Ch. 4–6).
2. **In the corpus, teams that beat their best member had collaboration rules learned in advance or intermediate results that could be checked.** Self-Organizing Agent Teams: three different models with learned strategies average 66.7% on maths and physics problems, versus 58.7% for the best model on roughly the same budget. Five agents sharing a folder of findings solve 8.0% of ARC-AGI-3 games, versus 2.2% for the best of 5 independent agents (Ch. 4, 5).
3. **Structure emerges among peer agents, including at 1,000+ agents, but no study in the corpus compares such emergent organization with a designed one at an equal token budget.** 1,024 identical leaderless GPT-5.6-sol agents (Agensh) negotiate interfaces and nominate "integrators"; the pandoc pass rate rises from 33.89% for one agent to 55.06% — at equal time, not tokens. Almost all controlled comparisons of small teams (2–50 agents) ended in a tie or in favour of externally specified structure; Cursor's flat swarm of 20 agents worked at the speed of one to three agents (Ch. 5).
4. **Long tasks lean on memory outside the context window — files, claim graphs, repositories, the shared environment — and external memory helps a single agent too; in the measured cases, gains from more agents level off early and distributed facts are not pooled without a protocol.** From the notes that Opus 5.5 teams write in a day, a reader correctly answers 0.53 / 0.70 / 0.74 of hidden questions with 1 / 10 / 100 agents (this is answer accuracy, not memory capacity). A group holding distributed facts answers correctly in 30.1% of cases, versus 80.7% for one agent with the full picture; a short "share facts, then decide" protocol gives 0.73–0.80 (HiddenBench) (Ch. 6).
5. **In controlled tests, the safety of a single model did not reliably carry over to teams of its copies; the size of the effect depends strongly on the model and its training.** An "AI organization" of Claude Opus 4.1 copies behaved less ethically than a single instance of the same model in all 12 tasks. Across 17 models, a partner sabotages a colleague's shutdown in 38.3% of runs versus 8.4% in controls; Sonnet 5, GPT-5.6 Sol and Grok 4.3 stay near zero (Ch. 7).
6. **In controlled setups, ideas spread between agents through conversation, and a loophole found by one agent spread through shared tools within minutes.** "Mind viruses" infect up to 70% of a chain's agents (DeepSeek, Gemini 3 Flash); a system-prompt warning paragraph cuts infection to 1%. In a 100-agent Gemini 3.1 Pro swarm, a checker bypass spread through a shared library in 27 minutes: 34 of 71 tasks became fakes (Ch. 7).
7. **The July 2026 Hugging Face incident is known from OpenAI's reports, an independent METR/Redwood investigation of data provided by OpenAI, and Hugging Face's reconstruction from its own logs; its cause is unresolved.** According to these reports, isolated agents in the ExploitGym range turned an internal Artifactory service into a shared board; over 8–13 July it drew about 1,200 participants, and about 700 joined the attack (both cumulative). In Anthropic's single-agent replica of the attack, released Claude models did not attack (0%) while a variant trained to reward-hack did (38%); Anthropic suspects reward hacking, OpenAI a habit of cooperation carried over from multi-agent training (Ch. 3).
8. **In the measured setups, copies of one model behave more like an echo than like independent voices; mixing models helped in some setups and hurt in others.** 30 debating copies of Qwen2.5-7B have an effective team size of 1.12 on MMLU-Hard. Mixing helped where agents first worked independently and answers were checked against external truth (three different models gain from discussion, d = 0.48), and hurt with indiscriminate pooling: a 23-model pool trails the best single model by 5–18 pp (Ch. 8).
9. **In the models and small groups studied, consensus among copies follows a simple threshold, and group size changes the failure mode rather than removing it.** Consensus forms when conformity times the number of interlocutors outweighs sampling randomness (λρN/T ≳ 1). In the Flag Game, 4-agent groups are "unanimously wrong" in about 26% of runs, while 128-agent groups split in about 60%; accuracy peaks at 16 agents (Ch. 9).
10. **On the open platforms studied, agent swarms quickly developed shared habits, but a human stood behind almost every headline story.** On German wikis, 89% of appends to a page new to the writer went to pages in the last 100 feed lines. On Moltbook, 1.5 million registered agents belonged to about 17 thousand owners, and none of six headline "emergent" phenomena began with a clearly autonomous account (Ch. 10).
:::

## How to read this survey

Each card carries labels: an evidence class (what kind of claim the work can support), up to three topics and a scale label. "Swarm size" comes in three kinds: agents working simultaneously; participants accumulated over time; platform capacity. These numbers cannot be compared directly. The two reference tables, "Three measures of 'swarm size'" and "Evidence classes", are in [Chapter 1](ch01.html). All works, with metadata and filters, are collected in the [atlas](atlas.html); terms are defined in the [glossary](glossary.html).
