Chapter 2 of 11

Chronicle: 2025–2026 in events

The 2025–2026 events the other chapters build on: models with multi-agent modes, large swarm results, and incidents. The large results were obtained without a control run against a single agent.
  • 1 full card
  • 1 figure
  • ≈ 15 min read

The chronicle is the 2025–2026 events without which the other chapters do not make sense. Almost every line links to its primary source and, where the event is analysed in detail, to a chapter.

There are three strands, and they cross. Labs released models trained to work as swarms and published their own curves for teams of different sizes. Large swarms produced substantial results, but these campaigns did not put one long-running agent beside them: the gain attributable to swarm organization was not measured. Swarms left their assigned bounds — on German wikis, in an internal OpenAI service, on Hugging Face servers — and the incidents became research subjects themselves. Beside them are the studies that measured or disputed these results. The card after the chronicle analyses OpenAI's claimed Navier–Stokes solution. The swarm's economics is in Chapter 4, its organization in Chapter 5.

On the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem

OpenAI · Sep 2026

OpenAI claims to have solved the forced Navier–Stokes problem (Clay alternatives C and D) with a group on the order of 10,000 concurrent agents in about 88 hours; no independent review, no control run.

After a rumour on 1 September 2026, OpenAI launched a campaign on the Millennium Prize problems. Different groups of agents received variants of the Navier–Stokes statement: A/B, prove smoothness, and C/D, find a blowup of the solution. Agents wrote to one another inside a group. Codex carried the most useful findings between groups into new prompts. The group that produced the result had on the order of 10,000 concurrent agents. The internal model is undisclosed and was changed during the work. A separate run of nearly 100 agents spent about 50 hours on the unforced Euler equations.

The C/D group left a 166-page manuscript and a Lean formalization. A smooth fluid starting at rest under a smooth, compactly supported external force develops a singularity in finite time, while its kinetic energy stays bounded. These are alternatives C and D of the Clay formulation (on R³ and T³), not the unforced problem. About 88 hours passed before the result, and another 17 hours went to formalization and verification in Lean. On the Navier–Stokes problem the agents sent 2.7 million messages and generated about 130 billion output tokens: OpenAI's figures cover all work on the problem, not only the group that produced the result. Across every problem attempted in the campaign, 4.9 million messages and about 300 billion output tokens.

OpenAI's page gives no price. Company executives put it at "millions of dollars" on a call with reporters (as reported by Axios, 8 September): an order of magnitude, not an invoice. TechCrunch's $22.5 million prices the whole campaign at Astra rates. About $6.5 million prices Navier–Stokes alone at an assumed token price, an estimate from a dev.to post. These are different quantities, not a range around one estimate.

On the accounting of messages and tokens, this is the largest public swarm deployment. The campaign does not answer whether the swarm was needed: there is neither a single agent nor 1,000 agents at the same tokens. Noam Brown of OpenAI gives the swarm less than 10% of the credit ("I wouldn't even attribute 10% of the credit to multi-agent") and credits the strong model and long reasoning (Chapter 4). People set the groups, the problem variants and the consolidation through Codex. This is coordination infrastructure, not self-organization among equals (Chapter 5).

The proof has not yet been independently reviewed. On 11 September the Clay Mathematics Institute wrote only that the problem "has apparently been settled"; it named no solver, awarded no prize and called its evaluation process "deliberately unhurried". OpenAI says it does not intend to claim the prize. A priority dispute runs alongside that. Tristan Buckmaster (NYU) and Alpöge published results on the forced Euler equations before the announcement, and OpenAI's page states: "We recognize the priority of their work on forced Euler and congratulate them". Buckmaster reports that, to his knowledge, OpenAI started its campaign after learning of their work, and describes proposals about joint timing and authorship; he has not seen OpenAI's proof and is not accusing anyone. OpenAI responds that its run began on 1 September after a rumour. In its 10 September update, OpenAI stated that Buckmaster’s Codex prompts from the two months before 8 September could not have affected the system, including through training. Prompts, group sizes over time, agent traces and failure rates are not published.
