Chapter 4

Swarm economics: what parallelism buys

What does a task gain from a swarm instead of one agent? In the lab measurements collected here, at equal total tokens a single agent was no worse than a swarm and the swarm won wall-clock time; quality gains above the single-agent ceiling were reported in specific settings — verifiable intermediate progress with shared findings, organization learned in advance, or a higher spend — and studies hold different things equal.

The number of agents can change three different things: time to solution, final quality and cost. So before comparing a swarm with a single agent, one has to state what was equalized: tokens, dollars or time. The evidence here is of three kinds: lab charts and system cards, controlled comparisons under a shared token budget, and high-profile campaigns with no single-agent control run. The table below summarizes the main comparisons together with their conditions. The chapter then covers the lab data, controlled comparisons, cases where communication between agents raises final quality, campaigns without a control, swarms with a trained orchestrator, and the theory of parallel labour.

Lab curves at equal tokens: the measured gain is time

Four sources with different methods: OpenAI's charts for GPT-5.6 Sol (1, 4 and 16 agents), Toby Ord's (Oxford) recasting of them into the labour-economics parameter λ, and the system cards of Claude Opus 5 and Opus 5.5 (single agent vs teams of 5 and 10; teams of 1–100 agents by best score after 2, 6 and 24 hours). As of 3 October 2026 we found no publications with swarm measurements from DeepSeek, Qwen (Alibaba), Zhipu (GLM), MiniMax or Mistral; the exception among labs of this group is Moonshot AI with the swarm mode in Kimi (section on reinforcement learning).

What λ is (the "stepping on each other's toes" effect)

Economists describe teamwork with one parameter: N workers get as much done as one worker working Nλ times as long. With λ = 1 the task divides perfectly, with no losses; with λ = 0.5 a hundred people equal one person working 10 times as long. Toby Ord carried the parameter over to agent swarms. On a plot of quality against total tokens (log axis) he drew a swarm line: from 1 to 4 to 16 agents at fixed tokens per agent. Its slope relative to the "one agent thinks longer" line is λ. Three rules follow from λ. A 10 times larger swarm gives as much as a single agent thinking 10λ ≈ 3–5 times longer. To match the effect of a 100-fold longer chain of reasoning in a single agent, the swarm must grow 900–15,000 times. On the other hand, N agents can finish roughly Nλ times faster at N1−λ times the spend: with λ ≈ 0.5, sixteen agents give about a quarter of the time for four times the cost. All values come from one model, one architecture (a lead agent plus subagents) and at most 16 agents; nobody has measured what happens with thousands.

Swarm Scaling

From the points of OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol charts, Ord estimated λ = 0.48–0.68: at equal tokens one agent beats a swarm, and a swarm of N agents equals a single agent thinking Nλ times longer.

What they did. Ord (post of 21 September 2026) took the interactive charts from the GPT-5.6 Sol launch post, where one model solves BrowseComp, SEC-Bench Pro and Terminal-Bench 2.1 alone, as four agents and (on the first two) as sixteen agents at five effort levels, replotted them on a log scale of total output tokens across all agents, and drew a "swarm line" at fixed tokens per agent. The slope of this line relative to the "one agent thinks longer" line is λ; the regressions were run by Claude Opus 5.

Results.

  • Tokens. A 4-agent swarm needs about twice as many tokens as a single agent for the same result, and a 16-agent swarm about twice as many again.
  • λ. 0.68 on BrowseComp, 0.57 on SEC-Bench Pro and 0.48 on Terminal-Bench 2.1 (only 1 and 4 agents there). A 16 times larger swarm equals a single agent thinking 160.57 ≈ 4.9 times longer.
  • Time and money (our estimate, not Ord's). From OpenAI's simulated latencies at equal quality, 16 agents are 2–2.8 times faster than a single agent on SEC-Bench Pro and 3.8–4.6 times faster on BrowseComp, while 4 agents on Terminal-Bench 2.1 are 1.1–1.4 times faster. In dollars at API prices λ comes out higher: 0.54–0.71.

Why it matters. The estimate rests on the lab's own chart points: at equal tokens a single agent beats a swarm, and the swarm's main benefit is time. Ord allows that a large swarm's ceiling may turn out higher than a single agent's, but this has not yet been shown (the closest example, and an expensive one, is the Claude Opus 5 team on BrowseComp, below).

Caveats. A blog post, not a paper: regressions (specification not published) on 5 points per swarm size, one model, one rigid "lead plus subagents" architecture; OpenAI does not disclose the number of tasks or the variance; latency and cost are an offline simulation, and the runs are far from the context limit, so memory effects (Chapter 6) are not in the data. The author removed the section on the 10,000-agent swarm and Navier–Stokes on 27 September 2026: according to OpenAI staff, as relayed by two commenters, the chart showed a single agent's reasoning getting longer, not the swarm getting larger (OpenAI has not clarified this officially).

GPT-5.6 Sol launch: the 4-agent Ultra mode lifts BrowseComp from 90.4% to 92.2% at the cost of more tokens and money; chart points for 1, 4 and 16 agents are embedded in the page.

What they did. In the GPT-5.6 Sol launch post of 9 July 2026, OpenAI described the Ultra mode: by default four agents work in parallel, and a beta API lets developers launch subagents and synthesize their work in a single request. The interactive charts compare 1 agent, 4 (Ultra) and, for BrowseComp and SEC-Bench Pro, 16 agents at five effort levels; the axes are total output tokens of all agents, simulated cost at API prices, and simulated latency of the root agent.

Results. The launch table publishes only Sol/Ultra pairs: BrowseComp 90.4% → 92.2%, SEC-Bench Pro 71.2% → 74.3%, Terminal-Bench 2.1 88.8% → 91.9%. The gain comes with more tokens and higher cost. The chart points themselves, including the 16-agent ones, are embedded in the page as Vega-Lite data; Ord worked from them. The developer guide adds that GPT-5.6 was trained "end-to-end" with "native multi-agent orchestration", and that instructing the model on when to invoke subagents makes it more likely to spawn them only where the extra tokens would improve the result.

Why it matters. At equal total tokens these data show no swarm advantage (see Ord's card): the launch shows parallelism paying off in speed and a few points of quality. It is one of the few public datasets in which a frontier model is systematically run as a swarm of three sizes, and OpenAI's first product in which multi-agent operation is built into training rather than only into the harness.

Caveats. A marketing post without peer review: no number of tasks, runs or confidence intervals; latency and cost are an offline simulation based on production data (OpenAI itself notes that real results may differ substantially); latency is measured at the root agent and hides subagent queues; the architecture is a fixed "root plus subagents" hierarchy, not a free swarm.

Noam Brown – Agent swarms, alignment, & recursive self-improvement

Noam Brown (OpenAI): four agents finish roughly twice as fast at twice the cost; for the Navier–Stokes result he would not give the swarm even 10% of the credit.

Noam Brown (OpenAI), interviewed by Dwarkesh Patel on 17 September 2026, described swarm economics in words: on some benchmarks, "if you have four agents working on the problem, it is done twice as fast … you're paying 2x more to get an answer twice as quickly"; with 16 agents the picture is similar but slightly less efficient, and the speedup is "slightly sublinear, though it does depend a lot on the problem" (search and mathematics parallelize better than writing a novel). OpenAI's public measurements go up to 16 agents; for the swarm of about 10,000 agents that, according to OpenAI, solved the Navier–Stokes problem (Chapter 2), there is no single-agent control run ("we haven't done that experiment yet"), and Brown says "I wouldn't even attribute 10% of the credit to multi-agent", although "we think it helped". An interview, not data: no benchmark names, tokens or intervals.

What they did. Section 8.12 of the Claude Opus 5.5 system card (22 September 2026) covers teams made of copies of the model. Small teams: a single agent versus a team of 5 peer agents and versus a lead with subagents on ProgramBench (166 tasks: reconstruct a program's source from its binary and documentation) and DRACO (100 short research tasks). Large teams: 1, 10, 30 and 100 agents work for 24 hours without network access to build a knowledge base about a company and to prove a theorem in Lean; three attempts per point; comparison with Opus 5 and Fable 5.1.

Results.

  • ProgramBench. A team of 5 reaches 60% of hidden tests 2.7 times faster than a single agent but spends about 1.9 times as many tokens (our estimate from the chart); all three architectures share the same ceiling, around 97–98%.
  • DRACO. Without limits, teams are slower than a single agent because of coordination; at a budget of 0.5 a team of 5 scores no less than an unlimited single agent, about 2.8 times faster.
  • Large teams over 24 hours. Knowledge base (share of hidden questions a reader answers correctly from the team's notes alone): 0.53 / 0.70 / 0.71 / 0.74 for 1 / 10 / 30 / 100 agents; Lean: 0.39 / 0.66 / 0.66 / 0.68. Almost all of the gain comes from going from 1 to 10.
  • Model generation. 10 Opus 5.5 agents in Lean (0.66) beat 100 agents of Opus 5 (0.58) and Fable 5.1 (0.53).
  • After 2 hours. 100 agents score 0.44 in Lean, a single agent 0.07.

Why it matters. This is a quality-versus-tokens curve for a frontier-model team from the developer itself: the team buys time with tokens, the final ProgramBench level is the same, returns to team size fall off quickly, and a new model generation gives more than a tenfold larger team. The large teams are compared at equal time, not equal cost (see Chapters 5 and 6).

Caveats. Self-report without peer review; the large teams ran on internal tasks and on "a slightly different model version than the released one", multi-agent ProgramBench on "an alternate, broadly comparable snapshot". Confidence intervals exist only for ProgramBench (166 tasks, 95% bands from between-task variance); the large teams have two tasks, three attempts per point and no intervals at all, DRACO is a mean of two runs. Tokens and dollars for the 24-hour runs are not disclosed, "time" is computed from tokens, and the single agent had not plateaued after 24 hours.

What they did. In Section 8.11 of the Claude Opus 5 system card (24 July 2026), Anthropic compared one agent with teams of model copies on two benchmarks: BrowseComp (find a hard-to-find fact on the web) and ProgramBench. Architectures: 5 or 10 peer agents with messaging and a lead, or a lead with asynchronous helpers; each agent had a budget of 1 million tokens, while the single agent in search got 1, 3 or 10 million with history compaction. Time is computed, cost is at API prices with perfect caching.

Results.

  • BrowseComp. All team architectures are no worse than the best single agent, and the team of 10 agents gave the best result: 93.6% versus 90.5% for a single agent with a 10-million-token budget (+3.1 points); raising that agent's budget from 3 to 10 million added 0.7 points.
  • Cost. The gain came at an equal nominal token budget (10 × 1 million versus 10 million for the best single agent) but at roughly 2.5 times the actual spend: in Anthropic's words, teams shift the quality–cost frontier "up and to the right"; from the chart, a task costs the 10-agent team about $95 versus $37. Going from 5 to 10 agents added 0.9 points at about 1.6 times the cost.
  • ProgramBench. A team of 5 reaches 60% of hidden tests 2.2 times faster than a single agent while spending about 2.5 times as many tokens, and its higher final score is explained mainly by a five times larger total budget (the single agent had 1 million tokens).

Why it matters. In search, the team raises the ceiling, not only the speed: it goes above a level the single agent did not reach at any budget tried, and pays for this in money. In programming, the team buys only speed: in the Opus 5.5 card the single agent was given 20 million tokens, the final levels converged, and the speedup grew to 2.7 times (both the model and the budgets changed).

Caveats. A pre-release model version with unpublished settings and without safeguard classifiers; the authors call the numbers relative. For BrowseComp, the number of questions, runs and confidence intervals is not given (ProgramBench has 166 tasks and 95% bands from between-task variance), and BrowseComp tasks with leakage found after the fact were scored as wrong (how many is not stated). Prices and tokens not in the card's text are our estimates from the charts.

Agent swarms and the new model economics

Cursor: a swarm wrote SQLite in Rust for $1,339 with a model mix versus $10,565 with GPT-5.5 in every role, with similar results; there is no single-agent control.

Cursor (Wilson Lin, blog post of 20 July 2026) gave its swarm the task of writing SQLite in Rust from the 835-page manual without source code or internet access, and compared the new version of the swarm with the old one using the same models and a four-hour limit (the design of both versions and the story of the flat swarm's failure are in Chapter 5). By the fourth hour the new runs passed 73–85% of sqllogictest tests versus 11–77% for the old ones, and later reached 100%. Here the economics are set by the model mix (the post does not disclose the number of agents): $1,339 for an Opus 4.8 planner with Composer 2.5 workers versus $10,565 for GPT-5.5 in every role, with similar results — almost an eightfold price difference for the same outcome. There is no "one long agent" control, one run per configuration; "context efficiency, more than parallelism" is the author's hypothesis (Chapter 6).

Controlled comparisons at equal budget

In these studies the team and the single agent get the same tasks, the same tools and a shared token budget that the team splits among its members; actual spend may differ. They fill the gap in the lab data, where swarm and single agent rarely get the same budget. The team's effect is set mainly by the task: the team wins where the task naturally splits into parts and loses where each step depends on the previous one; a mean effect of zero hides a wide spread (Kim et al. card). The largest of these comparisons, 260 configurations, is peer-reviewed in Nature Machine Intelligence.

What they did. Google Research, Google DeepMind and MIT (Yubin Kim and co-authors) compared one agent with three-agent teams in four architectures (independent workers, a coordinator, peers with voting, a hybrid) on the same tasks, with the same tools and a shared token budget that the team split among its members. In total, 260 configurations: six agentic benchmarks (web search, financial analysis, planning in Minecraft, office tasks, code repair, terminal) and nine 2025 models from GPT-5-nano to GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro and Claude Sonnet 4.5 (eight on code repair and terminal). The preprint appeared on 9 December 2025, the peer-reviewed version in Nature Machine Intelligence on 24 July 2026.

Results.

  • Mean and spread. The team's mean gain over a single agent across 6 benchmarks and 4 architectures is 0.0%, with a 95% CI from −58.7 to +77.2% and a range from +80.8% (financial analysis with a coordinator: 63.1% vs 34.9%) to −70.0% (step-by-step planning in PlanCraft with independent agents: 17.0% vs 56.8%). On code repair, every team is worse than the single agent.
  • Selection rule. The rule that extra agents will not help once a single agent solves more than ~45% of tasks predicted the sign of the effect in 15 of 16 held-out cases; the authors call it a practical selection rule, not a law.
  • Cost of coordination. Reasoning steps rise by 58% (independent), 263% (peers), 285% (coordinator) and 515% (hybrid); successes per 1,000 tokens are 2.8–5 times lower for the three architectures with message exchange (23.9 / 21.5 / 13.6 versus 67.7).
  • Errors. Without a verifying component, errors multiply: at the level of logs, independent agents amplify them 17.2 times; in final answers the difference is about 1.1 times.

Why it matters. A peer-reviewed comparison at equal compute budgets (not equal actual token spend): the zero mean here means heterogeneity, not absence of an effect. On individual tasks the team wins or loses by a lot, and the sign is set mainly by the task and by whether the architecture fits it. Hence a selection rule: the better a single agent handles the task alone, the less sense a team makes.

Caveats. The budget is small (about 4,800 reasoning tokens per task), teams of three (up to nine in one experiment), 2025 models, predefined architectures only; on SWE-bench and Terminal-Bench 20 tasks per cell (about ±20 pp), one run per configuration; only the single-agent result passes a strict statistical test.

The Illusion of Multi-Agent Advantage

Six automatically built multi-agent systems fail to beat voting over five reasoning chains in 10 of 18 dataset–model pairs, at a median cost 1.7 times higher.

Salesforce Research with HKUST (Guangzhou), UBC and NTU (preprint of 11 June 2026) compared six automatically built multi-agent systems (DyLAN, MAS-Zero, ADAS, AFlow, MaAS, MAS-Orchestra) with voting over five reasoning chains on GPQA-Diamond, HLE-Maths, SWE-Bench Lite, BrowseComp-Plus and their own SMFR task. In 10 of 18 dataset–model pairs no system beats voting (including all four on BrowseComp-Plus); the median cost is 1.7 times higher, and for 14% of pairs 10 times or more. Agents in the generated systems converge immediately (with GPT-5, in more than 90% of DyLAN cases), and architecture search often rediscovers self-consistency. Manual decomposition on SMFR lifts GPT-5 from 57.0% to 96.5% at 1.16 times the cost: structure helps when a human builds it for the task. Decentralized teams are excluded.

Debate or Vote: Which Yields Better Decisions in Multi-Agent Large Language Models?

Voting over five independent answers without discussion is more accurate than multi-agent debate (0.769 vs 0.738 for Qwen2.5-7B); the benefit comes from the number of independent attempts, not from communication.

Details
Authors
Hyeong Kyu Choi, Xiaojin Zhu, Sharon Li
Organizations

The University of Wisconsin–Madison (NeurIPS 2025, spotlight; preprint August 2025) split "multi-agent debate" into two parts: five independent answers with voting, and rounds of discussion. On seven task sets, for Qwen2.5-7B voting without discussion gives 0.769, the best debate variant 0.738 and a single agent 0.721; for Llama3.1-8B, 0.724 / 0.699 / 0.620; centralized debate is worse than a single agent, and extra rounds do not help. For an idealized model, the authors prove that the expected belief in the correct answer does not change during discussion (a martingale): the benefit comes from the number of independent attempts, not from communication. 2024 models, short answers, identical agents; for current swarms this gives theory, not a measurement.

Where communication raises final quality

Almost all of the controlled comparisons above either forbid agents to communicate while working or give them short tasks with nothing to divide. What they did. Microsoft Research (AI Frontiers) with UC Berkeley (Park, Papailiopoulos and co-authors; preprint of 17 September 2026) contrasted two ways of spending k times more compute: run k agents independently and take the best result (best@k), or let the same k agents work simultaneously in one container, sharing findings through a shared folder of logs, with no roles and no boss. Results.

  • ARC-AGI-3. A team of 3 solves 4.6% of games versus 1.4% for best@3, a team of 5 solves 8.0% versus 2.2% (a single agent about 0.5%); catching up takes 13 and 33 independent agents. None of the 64 single-agent runs beat the LP85 game; a team of 5 beats it in 65% of runs.
  • Tokens. The team spends more output tokens but reaches a given level sooner: independent agents need 3.8–4.9 times more tokens to match its final result; at a small budget (under ~400 thousand tokens per agent) the independent agents lead. On two games a team of 5 also beat a single agent with five times the action budget, while a team squeezed into a single agent's budget lost.

Why it matters. A controlled comparison with the same number of agents and the same action budget per agent: the team with a shared folder beats the best of the independent runs on final quality, not only on speed, although it spends more output tokens. The conditions are narrow: the gain appears where intermediate progress is measurable and the budget is sufficient.

Caveats. A preprint; the absolute ARC numbers are tiny and the gain rests on 4 of 25 games; for polyomino and MNIST the best runs are shown, and there is a single MNIST team; only output tokens are counted; on Terminal-Bench 2.0, where progress is hard to verify, a team of two did not beat two independent agents; the model is deliberately not the strongest.

Moonshot AI (Kimi Team; K2.5 technical report on arXiv 2 February 2026, model and blog post 27 January) described the Agent Swarm mode: one copy of the model acts as a "conductor" (orchestrator), deciding how many helpers to create, what prompts to write for them and which subtasks to hand out in parallel; the helpers do not talk to each other and each returns a single message.   • Speed. According to the report, on WideSearch the swarm reaches a target Item-F1 (30–70%) 3.0–4.5 times faster than a single agent; over training, accuracy rises from about 37% to 64%, and the average number of concurrent helpers from ~8 to ~14.

Caveats. Self-report without replication or ablations (the contribution of RL and of each bonus is not isolated); the speedup is shown on one benchmark in nominal "critical steps", and the swarm was tested only on search tasks and a closed internal benchmark built for parallelization. The limits of up to 100 helpers and 1,500 steps (K2.5) and 300 and 4,000 (K2.6) are claims from blog posts, not measurements; there are no safety evaluations of the swarm.

Theory: how much parallel labour can deliver

Ord's λ estimates (0.48–0.68) match the assumptions of "intelligence explosion" models: the AI Futures Model uses λ = 0.5, Davidson and Houlden 0.6. Ord notes that he got just the values these models assumed, although he had "hoped that the value of λ for AI agents would be lower". In these models λ determines whether AI researchers working in parallel can accelerate progress up to a vertical asymptote. Two 2026 works approach the question from different sides: Trammell shows that without growth in "parallelization technology" an explosion is not guaranteed, while Bradshaw bets that organized communication will make returns superlinear.

Takeaways

  • Lab data: at equal total tokens a single GPT-5.6 Sol agent is no worse than a swarm (λ = 0.48–0.68, Ord); an Opus 5.5 team reaches the same level faster but spends more tokens (Opus 5.5 card). In these measurements the swarm's main benefit is time.
  • Under a shared token budget the team's effect is set by task and architecture, not by the number of agents: the 0.0% mean in Kim et al. is heterogeneity (95% CI −58.7…+77.2%), not absence of an effect.
  • Quality gains above the single-agent ceiling come with conditions; the one reported in a lab system card came at a price (Opus 5: +3.1 pp on BrowseComp at ≈2.5× cost); a new model generation gives more than a tenfold larger team (Opus 5.5, Lean). λ gives a rough advance estimate of the cost (see the box).
  • Communication raises the final result where intermediate progress is verifiable and agents share findings (Park et al., DeLM, CORAL). Debate loses to voting over independent answers (Wisconsin), and automatically built systems usually do not beat voting (Salesforce).
  • Open: no work in the corpus compares a trained swarm (Kimi K2.5, GPT-5.6) with one long-running agent at equal tokens; high-profile campaigns (Navier–Stokes, zeta function, HAWK) run without a single-agent control; swarms of thousands of agents have not been measured.
