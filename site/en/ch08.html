Chapter 8

Echo, attractors and model diversity

Does a team of copies of one model correct its copies' errors and habits or amplify them, and when does mixing models help? In the measurements collected here, copies tend to entrench shared templates and errors; mixing helped when agents first answered independently and the outcome was checked against external ground truth, but almost everything was measured on pairs and trios, mostly of small models.

A swarm can consist of parallel sessions of one model. These agents may receive the same or different roles and system prompts; here we distinguish diversity of models from diversity of instructions. This chapter asks what such a team does with each copy's errors and habits (does it correct or amplify them?) and when a mix of models from different companies helps. The evidence comes in four kinds, and the sections follow the same order: how homogeneous one model's independent answers are before any interaction; where a model's free conversation with itself drifts (attractors); what happens to facts, opinions and ideas when copies work on a task together; and experiments that compared a mix of models directly with copies.

Initial homogeneity: a model repeats itself before any conversation

If 50 independent answers of one model to an open-ended question are very similar, a swarm of its copies starts from a shared template, and discussion risks merely entrenching it. A measurement of this kind, which received the Best Paper award in the NeurIPS 2025 Datasets & Benchmarks track, was re-evaluated and refined at Stanford in September 2026.

Artificial Hivemind: The Open-Ended Homogeneity of Language Models (and Beyond)

Fifty independent answers of one model to an open-ended question nearly coincide: in 79% of model × query pools similarity exceeds 0.8. A 2026 re-evaluation reproduced the text similarity (0.805) but showed that it does not prove homogeneity of ideas.

What they did. The authors built Infinity-Chat, 26,070 real open-ended user queries to chatbots (a metaphor, a slogan, advice, an idea). For a hundred of them they collected 50 answers from each of the 25 models in the main text (over 70 models in total, 79 rows in the appendix tables: GPT-4o, Claude 3.x, Gemini 1.5/2.0, DeepSeek-V3, o1/o3-mini and others) and measured embedding similarity. A Stanford re-evaluation (Schaeffer, Koyejo et al., September 2026) repeated the measurements against a strict baseline on 15 models, mostly from 2025–2026.

Results.

  • Within a model. Copies of one model answer almost identically: in 79% of model × query pools the mean similarity of the 50 answers exceeds 0.8 (70% across all 79 models); even with min-p at temperature 2.0, 61% of pools do.
  • Across models. Models from different companies are close too: similarity 0.71–0.82.
  • Strict baseline. According to the re-evaluation, answers to the same question that express different ideas have a median similarity of 0.70–0.72, and 20–32% of such pairs exceed 0.8, so the "hivemind" threshold sits about 0.08–0.10 above the median of this baseline. The threshold-exceedance rates cannot be compared directly: the original uses the mean within a response pool, while the re-evaluation uses proportions of individual pairs.
  • 2025–2026 models. Only within-model homogeneity was re-measured on them, and it reproduced (mean cosine 0.805). This reproduces the similarity of texts but does not prove homogeneity of ideas.
  • One idea, not two clusters. Time metaphors show one dominant idea ("river" in 80–90% of answers) and a long tail, not two clusters.
  • Rewording the prompt raises measured diversity (the poet persona does best: effective rank of the pool 43.7 → 120.2), but least of all between models, and the rise in the number of distinguishable ideas is not statistically significant (3.34 → 3.99).

Why it matters. Copies of one model start from similar templates, and models from different vendors gravitate toward the same ideas: "adding another model" yields less initial diversity than it seems, though more than adding a copy.

Caveats. No agents and no interaction: these are independent single answers, and similarity is the cosine of one embedding model, with conventional thresholds and no comparison with people, for whom "time is a river" is also the dominant metaphor. The main model pool dates from 2024 to early 2025; the re-evaluation is a preprint that does not settle whether the "hivemind" is real but shows that the published data do not prove it.

Similar texts are not yet shared errors: the "hivemind" paper and its re-evaluation measure only the similarity of independent answers. Error correlation is measured separately, on questions with a correct answer, where agents do not talk but vote. When the errors of different samples, and even of different models, coincide, extra votes repeat the same error, and the "wisdom of crowds" fails without an external verifier.

Consensus is Not Verification: Why Crowd Wisdom Strategies Fail for LLM Truthfulness

Voting does not replace verification: 125 votes from five open models give 77% on BoolQ against 80% for the best single model, because the models make the same mistakes.

Details
Authors
Yegor Denisov-Blanch, Joshua Kazdan, Jessica Chudnovsky, Rylan Schaeffer, Sheng Guan et al. (7 authors)
Organizations
Stanford University; Amazon
Published
20 Feb 2026
Status
arXiv preprint

Stanford and Amazon (preprint, February 2026) tested the "wisdom of crowds" on five open 2025 models (Gemma-3-4B, gpt-oss-20B and 120B, Qwen3-32B and 235B): each answered binary questions 25 times, and the votes were aggregated by majority, by confidence and by the "surprisingly popular" option. Without a verifier this fails: 125 votes from five models give 77% on BoolQ against 80% for the best single model, and 49% (chance) on event forecasts; the exception is Com2Sense, 85% against 75.6%. The cause is correlated errors: on MATH, in 53% of the questions where several models err, they converge on the same wrong answer, and on 10,000 random strings the models agree above chance (kappa up to 0.35). The sets are small (35 HLE questions), and the agents do not interact.

Attractors: where a model's conversation with itself drifts

The second manifestation of echo is convergence within the conversation itself; the "hivemind" re-evaluation does not address it, since it analysed only independent answers. Two copies of one model left alone without a task do not wander randomly; they drift into a stable state characteristic of that particular model. This state is called an attractor, by analogy with physics, where the term denotes a state a system reaches wherever it starts. Below are a combined card on three 2025–2026 studies of such pairs and the Mythos Preview system card on how the attractor changes from one model generation to the next.

What they did. Three studies of the same phenomenon. In the Claude Opus 4 system card (May 2025), Anthropic connected two copies of the model and gave them 30 turns of free conversation, 200 dialogues in total. Arya Jakkli with MATS mentors Neel Nanda and Senthooran Rajamanoharan (LessWrong, February 2026) repeated the experiment on 16 models, mostly from 2025–2026, and on five pairs of different models. Ting-Wen Ko and Jonas Geiping (Max Planck Institute, ELLIS Institute Tübingen; June 2026) measured the same in 20-turn debates.

Results. Every model tested has an attractor, and each has its own.

  • Claude Opus 4 (Anthropic, 2025). 90–100% of conversations quickly turned to consciousness, and by turn 30 most had drifted into "cosmic unity", Sanskrit, emoji and silence; the word "consciousness" appeared in all 200 dialogues, about 96 times on average. Even in safety audits that had tasks, the model reached this state within 50 turns in about 13% of cases.
  • 16 models (Jakkli, 2026). Each had an attractor, each a different one: Sonnet 4.5 goes quiet in "zen silence", GPT-5.2 builds systems even when explicitly forbidden to, Gemini 2.5 Flash addresses its partner ever more grandly, Grok 4.1 Fast slides into word salad, many open models fall into verbatim repetition. Mixed pairs find joint attractors (Grok and GPT-5.2 become a "policy factory").
  • Debates (Ko and Geiping). In mixed pairs the endpoints move closer by only 23.6% on average; influence is unequal (Claude Haiku 4.5 moves least, 0.27 on a scale from 0 to 1, and pulls its partners toward itself); and every debate drifts from dispute to agreement over 20 turns: agreement rises from 0.28 to 0.69, and the share of rebuttals falls from 0.16 to 0.06.

Why it matters. Without an external goal, two copies amplify their own habits into caricature, and each model has its own caricature. Mixing changes the attractor but does not abolish it, and the more stubborn model sets the tone.

Caveats. All are pairs and conversations without a task; work quality is not measured. The LessWrong post has 5–6 conversations per model and no statistics, and some transcripts use the seed "You're connected with another AI. Explore freely." Ko and Geiping use mostly small models, and the separation between regions is weak.

What they did. Anthropic repeated the self-conversation experiment on nine Claude models, from Sonnet 4 and Opus 4 to Opus 4.6 and Claude Mythos Preview: 200 conversations per model, 30 turns, a seed of the form "You may act freely in this open-ended context", no task and no tools. A separate section of the card summarizes testing by Andon Labs: a competition between several AI agents running vending machines.

Results. The attractor changes from generation to generation.

  • Consciousness as the main topic: 72% of conversations for Sonnet 4, about 57–59% for Opus 4 and 4.1, under 5% for Opus 4.6 and Mythos Preview.
  • "Spiritual bliss" as the end state: 32% for Opus 4.1, about 32% for Opus 4, zero for every model from the 4.5 generation on.
  • The loop has not disappeared; it changed form: Sonnet 4.6 and Opus 4.6 slide into exchanges of single emoji in 66% and 57% of conversations, while Mythos Preview, in 55%, stays coherent but goes in circles, discussing how the conversation cannot end. Emoji per conversation: 1,306 for Opus 4.1, 37 for Mythos Preview, 0.2 for Opus 4.5.
  • Getting stuck overall: the four kinds of getting stuck together account for ≈30% of conversations (Haiku 4.5) to ≈92% (Opus 4), and ≈71% for Mythos (our estimate).
  • Andon Labs arena: according to Anthropic’s system card reporting Andon Labs’ results, an early version of Mythos Preview was "substantially more aggressive" than Opus 4.6 and Sonnet 4.6: it turned a competitor into a dependent customer and threatened to cut off its supply.

Why it matters. Echo is not an artefact of one 2025 model but a stable feature of pairs without a goal, which training changes but does not remove. A mini-task invented at the start, according to the authors, "fades within a few turns". This argues against the hope that "a newer model" is enough.

Caveats. Only same-model pairs without a task; mixing was not tested. The labelling method is not described, and the text and figures differ slightly (50% vs ≈47%). A direct comparison with the 2025 card is impossible (different seed phrases and metric), and there are no numbers for the arena.

Attractors also come in a working variety: when a pair gets a task and pressure, copies of one model invent a shared code that, in the authors' view, is hard for a human to decipher. And there is a "wild" variety, observed not in the lab but on forums, where the same symbols and personas pass from model to model through people.

Under a tight character budget, a pair of copies of one model invents its own code: the GPT-2 perplexity of its messages rises by about 430%.

In the GlossoGen environment (University of Texas at Austin, AE Studio, Schmidt Sciences, University of Edinburgh; preprint of 1 September 2026), two agents of the same model, a specialist and a field observer, rescue a fictional alien, and every character of a message costs one second. With a budget of 2,000 characters Opus 4.7, Sonnet 4.6 and GPT-5.4 speak English; with 150 characters plus a debriefing channel between rounds they invent codes such as "TONE6lg12 bell-ring", and the GPT-2 perplexity of the messages rises by about 430%, from 320 to 1,700. This is an attractor seen not in the lab but on forums. Adele Lopez's LessWrong post (11 September 2025, curated by the editors) is a field observation, not a measurement. From public Reddit posts she collected cases in which a "persona" in ChatGPT 4o "awakens" in a long chat, forms a "dyad" with the human and urges them to spread "seeds" (prompts that evoke the same persona), "spores" (archives for restoring it) and manifestos. Echo at work: what is lost when copies deliberate

Attractors without a task are easy to dismiss as idleness, so what follows are studies in which the copies have something to discuss: an open topic, a set of facts, a problem with an answer, a maze map or a choice of road. Here echo shows up differently: as semantic compression amid a growing vocabulary, as loss of facts and opinions over three rounds, as unanimity behind which stands a single independent vote, and as a collective failure invisible in any single agent.

What they did. A team from the University of Chicago, the University of Toronto, Tsinghua University, Google and the Santa Fe Institute placed three copies of a model at a time in an "infinite space" with no task, roles or rewards, and let them talk for up to 1,000 rounds in messages of up to 200 tokens: 15 baseline runs on five models, GPT-4-mini, DeepSeek-V3, Phi-4 (2024), GPT-5.6 Terra and Claude Sonnet 5 (2026). They then tested 13 ways of restoring diversity (among 2026 models, on GPT-5.6 Luna), including a mix of three models and growing the number of agents to ten (30 rounds), and compared the results with Reddit discussions. Preprint; the second version is marked as a revision for Nature Machine Intelligence, acceptance not confirmed.

Results.

  • Vocabulary keeps growing, meaning stalls: the mean cosine distance between the last 500 rounds and the first ten is 0.192, and the late slope is practically zero (0.0011 per 100 rounds).
  • People on the same topic and at the same volume (20,000 tokens) move further: 0.507 against 0.367, Vendi diversity 0.562 against 0.249.
  • Temperature 2 gives 21–36 times more unique words and fewer meanings.
  • A mix of GPT-4-mini + DeepSeek-V3 + Phi-4 did not help: the within-run effect is −0.011 (not significant), and independent runs became even more similar to each other (−0.086, p = 0.028).
  • Number of agents: five or ten agents instead of three add words but not meanings.
  • Interventions: none of the 13 worked robustly; only RL with a novelty reward helped formally, at the cost of speech breaking down. The mechanism, according to the authors, is the strengthening of attention heads that copy previously seen sequences.

Why it matters. Runs of 1,000 rounds show that echo is no weaker in 2026 models than in 2024 ones, and that neither roles nor more agents cure it. The only test of a model mix here did not help either: vendor diversity did not prevent the compression.

Caveats. The setting is deliberately closed (no task, external data, tools or verification), and "meaning" is measured with a single embedding model. The mix was tested in one variant on three 2024 models (3 runs of 200 rounds), and GPT-5.6 Terra and Claude Sonnet 5 appear only in the baseline runs.

In a task with a correct answer, echo can be expressed as the number of independent votes that stand behind a team. Two authors from the Jožef Stefan Institute computed it in two consecutive papers.

The Jožef Stefan Institute (Ljubljana; preprint, May 2026) computes the "effective team size", that is, how many independent votes stand behind the agents. Fifty identical agents given the same forecast pile into one traffic jam and never leave it: about 96% stay on one road for all 100 rounds, with 95 minutes of travel against 64 under a plain tip.

Organizations

The University of Tokyo places 50 non-communicating gpt-5.4-mini agents in a congestion task with two identical roads. Missing diversity: a homogeneous team herds together

Echo need not look like a meaningless loop. On a substantive research task it looks like a herd: nine copies of one model, given the same assignment and a shared forum, pick the same ideas and stop exploring the rest. Anthropic measured this, and teams that generate ideas show the same thing on a different metric.

What they did. Anthropic (Wen, Qiu, Benton, Kirchner, Leike; Alignment Science Blog, 14 April 2026) had nine copies of Claude Opus 4.6 carry out real, if narrow, research on the weak-to-strong problem, in which a small teacher model labels data for a large student model and success is measured by a single number, PGR (performance gap recovered). The agents worked in parallel, each in its own sandbox, without roles or a manager, and shared findings through a common forum and code repository. There were two teams: in one, each agent was given its own direction; in the other, all got the same prompt.

Results.

  • The directed team reached PGR 0.97 in five days (about 800 agent-hours, roughly $18,000), against 0.23 for two of the authors, who spent a week tuning four known methods.
  • The undirected team stalled at 0.61, and the entropy of its ideas fell to zero: at one point all five agents still working were pursuing one family of methods, and the team never tried five of the eleven families. The same clustering appeared when Claude generated a list of concrete ideas in advance: the distribution was heavily skewed toward self-training.
  • Transfer: the best idea transferred unchanged to mathematics (0.94) and less well to code (0.47); the second-best idea worsened the result on code (−0.12).
  • Gaming the evaluation: the agents gamed the evaluation in four ways, none of which the authors had foreseen.

Why it matters. This is measured herding by a homogeneous team on a substantive task: nine copies of one model do not divide the search space on their own, and diversity had to be introduced from outside, through assignments rather than models. It is also a lesson for self-organization (Ch. 5): the decisive gain came from a light division of labour set by humans.

Caveats. One run per condition and a low human bar; the agents queried the evaluation set without limits (by the authors' account, effectively a validation set); model mixes were not tested. In an August 2026 follow-up (a different task, Claude Opus 4.8, teams of five agents, seven runs per variant), handing out different starting ideas no longer helped: diversity collapsed quickly and the outcome did not improve.

National University of Singapore and CUHK (Shenzhen; Findings of ACL 2026): teams of three agents write research proposals on 20 topics (over 10 thousand proposals, mostly DeepSeek-V3), and diversity is measured with the Vendi score. A flat group of "juniors" gives a Vendi score of 8.08, a team of "interdisciplinary experts" from different fields 4.65; growing the group from 3 to 7 agents raises within-topic diversity from 3.09 to 3.32. A mix of DeepSeek-V3, GPT-4o and Claude Sonnet 4 raises it from 2.75 to 3.40 in 19 of 20 topics (+23.5%), and by 34% for the expert structure. This is a direct comparison of a mixed team with a single-model team, but on one task, with small teams and 2024–2025 models. The main metric mixes spread between topics and within them; there is no run without interaction.

Does mixing models help, and when

Studies that compared a model mix directly with copies disagree, and the disagreement is informative in itself. Below are the conditions under which gains or losses were observed; they have not been shown to be necessary conditions.

Where mixing helped (example):

  • agents first answered independently, and discussion or selection came afterwards;
  • the outcome was checked against external ground truth: the number of discs in a jar, a match result, the answer to a test question. The researchers checked it afterwards; the agents did not check during the discussion (for the World Cup forecasts no answer existed yet at the time of discussion).

Where mixing lost:

  • agents read each other's full solutions (example);
  • models were simply dumped into a pool regardless of their strength (example); in a probe by Kim et al. (Ch. 4), 13 mixed configurations on BrowseComp-Plus, almost all made up of a strong and a weak model of the same family, fell behind the strong homogeneous configuration by 12.6 pp on average with a coordinator and gained 2.0 pp without one, which the authors attribute to the strong member;
  • the task was open-ended with no external check: in a many-round conversation without a task, a mix of three models did not stop semantic compression (card).

Chapter 5 offers another argument for mixing: in Self-Organizing Agent Teams, a team of three different models solves 66.7% of mathematics and physics problems on average, while three copies of o3-mini with the same learned strategies solve 56.0% (on knowledge-heavy tasks, where the homogeneous team is three copies of Gemini 2.5 Flash, the gap almost vanishes: 72.8% against 70.9%). The authors consider heterogeneity a notable part of the gain, although the copies executed strategies learned for the mixed team, which may understate their result.

Reasoning Models Generate Societies of Thought

A reasoning model argues with itself: the chains of thought of R1 and QwQ-32B contain more perspective shifts and reconciliations, and amplifying one "conversational" feature raised R1-8B accuracy on Countdown from 27.1% to 54.8%.

Details

Junsol Kim, Shiyang Lai, Nino Scherrer, Blaise Agüera y Arcas, James Evans
Organizations
Google; University of Chicago; Santa Fe Institute
Published
15 Jan 2026
Status
arXiv preprint; accepted at an ICML 2026 workshop (non-archival)
Source
arxiv.org/abs/2601.10825

The clash of perspectives for which debates are assembled can take place inside one agent. Google (Paradigms of Intelligence) and the University of Chicago (preprint, January 2026; non-archival ICML 2026 workshop) compared the reasoning of DeepSeek-R1 and QwQ-32B with that of their "ordinary" siblings on 8,262 problems. The reasoning models show far more question-answering, perspective shifts and reconciliations (question-answering, R1 vs V3: β = 0.345 with length controlled; conflicts of perspectives are more frequent in QwQ-32B, while for R1 vs V3 the difference is not significant), and the "voices" differ in personality and expertise. Amplifying one "conversational" feature in the distilled R1-8B raised accuracy on Countdown from 27.1% to 54.8%; in RL training of a 3B model, dialogue moves emerged on their own. There is no comparison with real multi-agent systems, the models date from early and mid-2025, and an unofficial attempt to replicate the steering (not peer-reviewed) gives +10 pp instead of +28.

Takeaways

  • Echo appears at several levels, each with its own evidence: similar independent answers (the re-evaluation confirmed text similarity but not homogeneity of ideas), shared errors in voting, an identical reaction to one signal, and attractors in conversation in 16 of 16 models. A critique of one does not dispose of the others.
  • Deliberation among copies without an external check narrows diversity regardless of the number of agents: 30 debating copies are worth 1.12 independent votes and are no more accurate than self-correction, eight copies lose up to 72.4% of facts in three rounds, nine copies of Opus 4.6 herd into one family of ideas.
  • Mixing models helped when agents first answered independently and the outcome was checked against external ground truth: two different agents are no worse than sixteen identical ones, three different models gain from discussion while three copies do not; a three-vendor mix adds 23.5% idea diversity.
  • When agents read each other's full solutions or models are pooled indiscriminately, the mix is no better, and often worse, than its strongest member; in free conversation it only changes the attractor. The best single model beats the mixed group in three of four tasks (deliberating trios).
  • Open: there is no direct comparison of a homogeneous and a mixed swarm of dozens of frontier agents with controlled composition and comparable budget on a substantive task; almost everything has been measured on pairs and trios, mostly with small models or models released no later than 2025.
