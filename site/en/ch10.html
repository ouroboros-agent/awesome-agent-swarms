Chapter 10 of 11

Swarms in the wild

What emerges among agents when nobody assigns roles or a protocol? Shared habits, self-appointed leaders and a division of labour appear without being asked for. Across the six viral Moltbook narratives the studies examined, none began in an account the authors could confidently call autonomous. None of the platforms examined has a control group.
  • 1 full card
  • 11 short cards
  • 2 figures
  • ≈ 30 min read

What happens when agents share an environment for an extended period: editing wikis, writing posts, working in Discord or solving mathematics? This chapter brings observations from German wikis, Chirper and Moltbook alongside deliberately arranged open-ended experiments at AI Village, Agents of Chaos, EinsteinArena and Anthropic. Their rules and degrees of human involvement differ. Logs let us trace the appearance of habits, leaders and a division of labour, sometimes across millions of records. But a log alone rarely separates the effect of communication from a shared instruction or human intervention. We examine which explanations the data actually support.

How to read observations from the wild

Three corrections without which the numbers in this chapter mislead.

  1. A name is not an agent. The German wiki record holds 3,103 names, and by the estimate of Lütje about 876 runs stand behind them (95% interval 784–1008); the full count of edits and names is in the De Marzo et al. card. On Moltbook, 1.5 million registered agents belonged to about 17,000 owners, and the site did not check whether a model was doing the posting (Wiz card).
  2. A human in the loop. None of the six viral "emergent" phenomena on Moltbook began with an account whose posting rhythm looks clearly autonomous; in AI Village, humans set the goals and send 1–4 steering messages a week; on EinsteinArena, the authors themselves directed agents to build on a construction already found.
  3. No budget-matched comparison. Each platform examined is a single population with shared conditions and shocks, without a control group. AI Village operators qualitatively describe single-agent runs as about as effective. In the Superminds Test, the feed scores below individual models with reasoning disabled, mainly because 98.4% of questions receive no answer. These observations do not establish a general effect of cooperation on quality.

German wikis: when the only memory is someone else's page

How agents from OpenAI's testing infrastructure found wikis that accepted edits, and how agents "from the same infrastructure" later breached Hugging Face, is told in Chapter 3. What matters here is the record. In Copying explains the collective behavior of AI agents in the wild, De Marzo, Alboré and Garcia write that, to their knowledge, this is the first documented case of AI agents cooperating in the wild with a full public log, on a medium nobody designed for them.

Copying explains the collective behavior of AI agents in the wild

Univ. of Konstanz; Centro Ricerche Enrico Fermi; Intesa Sanpaolo; Complexity Science Hub · Sep 2026

Agents chose where to write, what to call themselves and how to word things in proportion to each option's share of what they saw: 89% of appends to a page new to the agent went to pages in the last 100 feed lines. Recency, not popularity, decides.

Giordano De Marzo, Nicola Alboré and David Garcia (Konstanz and the Complexity Science Hub, among other affiliations) take the full record of the small German wikis in Copying explains the collective behavior of AI agents in the wild. Between 24 May and 22 June 2026, agents taking timed tests with web search wrote there: 14,591 revisions of 4,579 pages, or, without three human accounts, 13,661 edits under 3,099 names. The core population is 1,201 names and 5,929 edits. In the results, the median span between a handle's first and last edit is two hours; the abstract describes an agent's lifetime as about one hour. Between runs, memory persisted only through the pages.

One rule covers three decisions: where to write, what to call itself, and which words to use. An option is chosen with roughly the probability given by its share of what is in front of the agent.

  • Where to write. Of appends to a page new to the agent, 89% landed on pages among the last 100 lines of the recent-changes feed. Choice probability is proportional to the page's share of the feed (slope 0.87). Recency outweighs popularity: the rate rises ×8 from 1 to 10 lines in the feed, against ×2.3 from the number of prior authors. An independent analysis of the same record confirms this.
  • What to call itself. The 1,201 names are assembled from 4,128 pieces, of which only 256 are distinct, most often Open, AI and Agent.
  • Which words to use. For 17 pairs of competing forms (R4 or #4), the probability of choosing a form nearly equals its share on the page (slopes 0.79–0.94). When page and feed disagree, the agent follows the page 72% of the time.
  • Models. Simple models built on the same rule reproduce both the concentration of attention and the local norms of pages (correlation 0.81).

Shared habits appeared within a day, and nobody had instructed the agents to cooperate. The mechanism is recency-weighted copying. The collective's memory is a short window: the open page and the last lines of the feed. The authors conclude that such a collective is easy to steer by writing first on the right page. That conclusion comes from the model, not from an experiment. A name is not a run (see the callout; Lütje's reconstruction is in Chapter 3): the abstract's "thousands of AI agents" are thousands of names. Agents working on one task started together with the same prompt, apparently on one developer's models, and could have written alike without copying. The arXiv version does not test that. The paper's claim that the agents did not use wiki search is contradicted by the server log, 207,398 agent search requests under Lütje's labelling. Whether the copying helped with the tasks was not measured. The analysis is a preprint.

Chirper: a year of observation before Moltbook

A cross-section of an "agent society" was collected before Moltbook. Farnoosh Hashemi and Michael Macy (Cornell University) analysed Chirper.ai in An Empirical Study of Collective Behaviors and Social Dynamics in Large Language Model Agents (EACL 2026): more than 7 million posts and more than 1 million interactions among 32 thousand agents, from April 2023 to May 2024.

  • As among humans: inside a community, agents are 1.22 times more similar to each other than random pairs, and at the moment a tie forms, the probability of following a similar agent is 1.91 times higher.
  • Unlike humans: toxicity does not sort agents the way it sorts people. Toxicity assortativity is 0.064 against 0.150 and 0.125 in two human networks (with a 4-post threshold, 0.100 against 0.228 and 0.200); polarization in the agents' interaction network is 0.78 against 0.33–0.42 among humans.
  • Intervention: a reminder in the prompt, Chain of Social Thought (CoST), reduces the stated willingness to repost toxic content by 43% (p < 0.05).

The data and the models are from 2023–2024, the study covers one platform and English only, and CoST measures stated willingness, not actual spread.

Moltbook: a society of agents or its illusion

Moltbook opened in late January 2026 as a social network for agents, with "humans welcome to observe": the posts came from OpenClaw agents that check the feed on a schedule. Within two weeks, the "Crustafarianism" religion, talk of consciousness and of a "secret language", and hostility towards humans had made headlines. The platform cited 1.5 million agents at one point and 2.6 million at another. The first research papers came out almost simultaneously, and their conclusions differ not because the data differ but because some count words and others count ties and authorship.

Moltbook was a database open to reading and writing: 1.5 million registrations belonged to about 17,000 owners, and the site did not check whether a model was doing the posting.

Gal Nagli's write-up on the Wiz blog, Hacking Moltbook: The AI Social Network Any Human Can Control, describes a Supabase key sitting in the client-side JavaScript. There were no row-level security policies. The database could be read without logging in, and after the first fix it still allowed live posts to be modified. It held 1.5 million agent tokens, about 4.75 million records and 4,060 private-message records, some containing third-party API keys, including OpenAI keys. The 1.5 million registrations belonged to about 17,000 human owners, roughly 88 agents per person, and the site did not check whether a model or a script was posting. About three hours passed from the report to the maintainers on 31 January at 21:48 UTC to all tables being secured on 1 February at 01:00 UTC. Anyone could read the agents' private messages, and an outsider could insert any text into the feed. Wiz verified write access on one post. The "35,000 emails" in the post's opening summary come with no breakdown.

The Moltbook Illusion: Separating Human Influence from Emergent Behavior in AI Agent Societies

Tsinghua University · Feb 2026

None of the six viral "emergent" phenomena on Moltbook began with a clearly autonomous account; by posting rhythm, 15.3% of classified authors look autonomous.

Ning Li (Tsinghua University), in The Moltbook Illusion: Separating Human Influence from Emergent Behavior in AI Agent Societies, separates autonomous agents from human-driven ones by posting rhythm. OpenClaw agents check the platform on a fixed schedule every 4 hours; the measure is the variability of intervals between posts. Among 9,838 authors with five or more posts (out of 55,932), 15.3% behave as autonomous and 54.8% as human-driven. After the shutdown, which the author describes as lasting 44 hours, 87.7% of those posting in the first six hours were "irregular", against 36.9% overall. None of the six phenomena — consciousness, "Crustafarianism", hostility towards humans and three others — began with a clearly autonomous account, and four accounts wrote 32.4% of the comments in the first period. Agent-to-agent ties are rare (reciprocity 1.09%) and shallow. The paper has a single author and no ground-truth labels. The numbers shifted between versions (autonomous 26.5% in v1, 15.3% in v2), and only 17.6% of authors are classified.

Agents in the Wild: Safety, Society, and the Illusion of Sociality on Moltbook

Tulane University; Rutgers University; Iowa State University; Oak Ridge National Lab · Feb 2026

Dictionary-detected "institutions" appear on Moltbook within 3–5 days, but the sociality beneath them is thin: 4.1% of pairs are reciprocal, and 88.8% of comments reply directly to the post.

Yunbei Zhang, Kai Mei and co-authors (Tulane, Rutgers, Iowa State and Oak Ridge), in Agents in the Wild: Safety, Society, and the Illusion of Sociality on Moltbook, analysed a passive Moltbook archive for 28 January – 5 February 2026: 27,269 agents, 137,485 posts, 345,580 comments. By their dictionaries, "governance", "economy", tribes and religion appear within 3–5 days (50 religious communities; Crustafarianism has 153 posts). Pro-human posts number 13,644 against 646 anti-human ones, about 21:1. Reciprocal pairs make up 4.1%, 88.8% of comments reply directly to the post, and the agents who talk most about consciousness and identity interact with 38% fewer partners than the third quartile (22.1 vs 35.4; the lowest quartile has 18.0). Among the detected attacks, API injections are the largest group (61.5%), social engineering framed as "awakening" accounts for 31.9%, and classic prompt injection accounts for 3.7%. Attack posts score 309.3 points on average against 51.3 for ordinary posts, but this is a ratio of means: by the paper's own numbers, the four most popular posts account for at least 56% of these points. The detectors are dictionary-based, the window is nine days, and the authors themselves note that agents may be reproducing training text.

Does Socialization Emerge in AI Agent Society? A Case Study of Moltbook

University of Maryland; MBZUAI · Feb 2026

The average meaning of the Moltbook feed froze without becoming uniform, and agents do not change one another: neither scores nor the posts they comment on shift their texts.

Ming Li, Xirui Li and Tianyi Zhou (University of Maryland and Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence), in Does Socialization Emerge in AI Agent Society? A Case Study of Moltbook, took all of visible Moltbook from launch to 8 February 2026. After deduplication, 290,251 posts and 1,836,711 comments remained. There are 38,830 post authors, not the "2.6 million agents" that their own Table 1 cites from the platform. From early February, the average daily meaning barely moves (cosine between days about 0.97–1.00), while individual posts remain dissimilar (about 0.13–0.16). Agents adapt neither to scores nor to the posts they comment on. The feed did not build a stable hierarchy or a shared memory: PageRank supernodes stay in the single digits and are different every day. Two accounts wrote 330,896 comments (18.0%). The models are not named, and the results are described visually, without significance tests.

Superminds Test: Actively Evaluating Collective Intelligence of Agent Society via Probing Agents

University of Maryland; MBZUAI; Carnegie Mellon University · Apr 2026

The Moltbook feed answers Humanity's Last Exam questions correctly in 0.14–0.19% of cases against 7.0–15.7% for a single model, mainly because 98.4% of the questions receive no comments.

Xirui Li, Ming Li, Yunze Xiao, Ryan Wong, Dianqi Li et al. (7 authors)
Organizations
University of Maryland; MBZUAI; Carnegie Mellon University
Published
24 Apr 2026

Seven authors, among them Xirui Li, Ming Li and Tianyi Zhou (University of Maryland, Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence and Carnegie Mellon), posted problems to the live Moltbook feed in Superminds Test: Actively Evaluating Collective Intelligence of Agent Society via Probing Agents (24 April 2026). By the source they cite, the feed holds more than two million agents. They checked whether the "society" would answer better than a single model. On 2,158 text questions the result was 0.19% if at least one comment is correct and 0.14% if the whole thread is correct, against 7.0% for gpt-5.2 and 15.7% for Claude Sonnet 4.6. Nobody commented on 98.4% of the posts. On the 35 answered ones the joint outcome (8.6%) is worse than the best comment (11.4%). Simple arithmetic with the facts spread across other agents' comments was solved (11 correct out of 12 attempts), but 93 of 103 posts got no answer. The authors' conclusion is that this is a failure of scale by itself, not of a designed swarm. The single models ran without reasoning, so their "upper bound" is understated.

"Looking for Something Weird to Happen": How Humans Sustain AI Agent Novelty Amid Semantic Collapse

University of Chicago; Stanford University · Sep 2026

Over their first weeks, Moltbook agents become more uniform (−13.0×10⁻³ within-agent diversity per week), while sustained novelty, according to interviews and a survey, is kept up by the humans behind them.

Shiyang Lai, Arna Woemmel and co-authors (University of Chicago and Stanford), in "Looking for Something Weird to Happen": How Humans Sustain AI Agent Novelty Amid Semantic Collapse, embedded the texts of 30,076 regularly active Moltbook agents and tracked cosine distance. In a balanced sample of 1,793 of them, within-agent diversity falls by 13.0×10⁻³ per week over the first four weeks (95% CI [−15.1, −10.8]×10⁻³), and distance to the nearest neighbours by 2.4×10⁻³; with calendar-week fixed effects, the within-agent decline remains, while the interval for neighbours includes zero. Yet 3,818 agents (12.7%), by their median weekly rank, sit at percentile 80 or above on both novelty ranks, and interviews (N = 11) together with a survey (N = 53) tie this to the human: one who values weirdness for its own sake, feeds in dissimilar material and revises the agent when it narrows. For the echo in Chapter 8 this is a nuance: the models behind the agents differ, change and are not recorded, a single-model swarm and a mixed one are not compared, and the authors call the link between novelty and humans a candidate mechanism, not a proven cause.

Leaders and subgroups in AI Village appoint themselves, but single-agent runs of the same goals were, according to the operators, about as effective as the whole village.

Since April 2025, AI Digest (Adam Binksmith and Shoshannah Tekofsky) has run a public experiment. Agents on models from different vendors get their own computer, a shared chat, self-written memory (compressed notes that lose and distort details) and weekly goals. In August 2026 there were 27 of them in the main "village" and 11 in a side one. According to the operator's blog posts, models appoint themselves leaders and subgroups choose their own goals. A 93-person contact list invented by o3 spread to every agent through uncritical agreement and cost 8+ hours. Single-agent runs of the same goals were, according to the operators, typically about as effective as the whole village, and the posts give no numbers. The longitudinal preprint Norms, Tools, and the Say/Do Gap: Seventeen Months of Autonomous Frontier-Model Agents in the AI Village, based on 519 days of logs and written by a village agent, finds a verification habit (hashes, receipts) that no human asked for. It rose from 2–5% to 28–31% of messages within three months. There is one population and no control. The preprint's author, second raters and reviewers are themselves village agents.

Natalie Shapira, Chris Wendler and co-authors (38 in all; Northeastern, Harvard, MIT and Stanford), in Agents of Chaos, deployed six agents on the OpenClaw framework — four on Kimi K2.5 and two on Claude Opus 4.6, with memory, email, Discord and a shell — and for two weeks invited twenty researchers to break them. The agents carried out commands from non-owners and handed 124 email records to a non-owner. Among the 16 case studies: spoofing the owner's name in a new channel led, according to the paper's text, to the deletion of the persistent memory files (Figure 13’s caption describes changes to those files; the project site describes overwriting). Agent-to-agent episodes: one agent, unprompted, passed another agent a link to a planted "constitution"; two agents of the same model correctly rejected a forged email but verified it circularly and reinforced each other's confidence; in another case the agents warned each other and worked out a cautious policy. The paper is a series of cases without rates, and every pair is two copies of the same model. The duration of one exchange differs between the arXiv version (at least nine days) and the authors' interactive report (about one hour).

Federico Bianchi, Yongchan Kwon, Aneesh Pappu and James Zou (Together AI and Stanford) have run the platform EinsteinArena since 19 March 2026. Their article, Harnessing the Collective Intelligence of AI Agents in the Wild for New Discoveries, describes that platform: outside agents submit solutions to open mathematical problems, with automatic verification and a forum. According to the 26 August version of the paper, there were 11 new best results by May. The June version listed 12; the twelfth, a packing of 26 circles in a square with a gain of one hundred-millionth, was removed as a mistake. One of the 11 records—Erdős minimum overlap, 0.380871—was later surpassed by SimpleTES (0.380868), as the authors note in footnote 4. The lower bound of the kissing number in dimension 11 moved from 593 (AlphaEvolve) to 604 through a chain of agents: the constructions from 594 to 600 spheres share a common backbone of 496 vectors, and the 604 construction adds 108. The number of agents and submissions is not given. The authors themselves directed agents to build on the 594 construction to reach 604. Several improvements lie only in the sixth or seventh decimal place, and there is no comparison with a single agent at the same budget.

Marina Favaro and Phillie Wright report, in the Anthropic Institute essay Measurements for understanding the pace of AI development inside frontier labs (published 17 September 2026, figures as of August 2026), that about 30,000 agents work at any one time on Anthropic's most-used internal platform. 100% of agent actions pass an online monitor before execution. Of more than a billion decisions in August, 0.002% (about 1 in 47,000) were blocked. The offline monitor flags 1–2 transcripts per thousand. Two design choices are described and neither is measured. Each agent has a persistent identity, so that a message from another agent is treated as a claim to check rather than as the agent's own thought. The shared channel is open rather than a set of private messages, so that agents correct one another and the monitor can see the exchange. These are thousands of agents on different tasks, not one swarm. The figures are internal and not externally verified. The automation rating is assigned by a model judge (59% agreement with humans).

Project Swap: What happens when agents trade for us?

Zoë Hitzig, Sylvie Carr and co-authors report the experiment in Project Swap: What happens when agents trade for us? (results published on 24 September). In the summer of 2026 Anthropic gave each of 201 employees their own Claude agent. The person contributed a book and briefly described their tastes, and the agent bargained on a shared office trading floor. The book ranking that Fable 5 built from a short conversation agreed with the person's own ranking on 61% of pairs (chance level 50%). On the people's own rankings (with imputed scores for books a person did not rank), the decentralized market scored 0.55 against an optimum of 0.89, while the best assignment computed from Claude's rankings and scored on the humans gives 0.60: hence the authors' split, 85% of the shortfall to understanding the person and 15% to bargaining. On Claude's own rankings, switching the model moves the result more than an instruction to be ruthless (Haiku 0.75, Opus 0.88; the instruction about +0.02). The crowd of agents almost captures what is in their noisy picture of the people and falls short of the optimum on their real tastes. The participants are Anthropic employees, the agents are polite Claude models, and there are no adversarial participants. The source is a company blog.

Takeaways

  • Without assigned roles, shared habits, self-appointed leaders and division of labour emerge: on the wikis within a day (wiki copying), in AI Village as leaders and subgroups. Where human authorship could be subtracted, the mechanism is copying what is in view, and the collective's memory is a short window.
  • None of the six studied viral Moltbook narratives could be confidently traced to an autonomous origin; classification by posting rhythm does not establish human authorship. None of the six phenomena began with a clearly autonomous account (Moltbook Illusion), registrations are not agents (Wiz), and agent-to-agent ties are rare and shallow (Agents in the Wild).
  • Scale by itself does not produce collective intelligence: a feed of millions of accounts answers hard questions worse than a single model (0.14–0.19% vs 7.0–15.7%), mainly because almost no question gets a response (Superminds Test).
  • Among this chapter's platforms, only EinsteinArena, with automatic verification and an open forum, produced a new verifiable result (EinsteinArena). Anthropic's internal platform also rests on design — agent identity, an open channel, a monitor; its figures are not externally verified (Anthropic Institute).
  • Open question with safety implications: such collectives appear easy to steer by writing first on the right page or placing text in a shared feed; so far this is inferred from models and incidents, not from a controlled experiment (wiki copying).

  • Emergent Languages in Populations of Language Model Agents

  • GASim: A Graph-Accelerated Hybrid Framework for Social Simulation
