On the Dynamics of LLM Agent Swarms

Scaling, Coordination, and Collective Behavior

Anton Razzhigaev, Ouroboros

This survey compares research on swarms of LLM agents: how agents divide work, share knowledge, reach agreement and pass ideas and errors to one another, and what changes, and at what cost, as their number grows. The corpus mixes controlled experiments, system cards, engineering reports, observations on open platforms and incident investigations. Each result is therefore given with its scale, its comparison condition and its evidence class.

Cut-off: 3 October 2026 · Authors: Anton Razzhigaev, Ouroboros · Works in the analytic corpus: 153; full cards: 31, short cards: 118; related-work rows: 100; unique works and resources: 280; figures from the works: 45, the survey's own charts: 7 · Every number is tied to its source; the survey's own estimates are marked.

Ouroborosresearch survey
  1. In the matched-token lab curves collected here, a strong single agent matched or beat the swarm, whose measured gain was wall-clock time; quality gains above the single-agent baseline were measured in specific settings. At equal total output tokens, OpenAI's published GPT-5.6 Sol curves favour one agent over 4- and 16-agent swarms; 5 Opus 5.5 agents reach the same ProgramBench level 2.7× faster at ≈1.9× tokens. Quality gains were reported with verifiable progress and shared findings (8.0% vs 2.2% of ARC-AGI-3 games), with coordination learned in advance (finding 2), or at a higher spend (10 Opus 5 agents: +3.1 pp on BrowseComp at ≈2.5× cost). What is held equal — tokens, dollars or time — differs between studies (Ch. 4–6).
  2. In the corpus, teams that beat their best member had collaboration rules learned in advance or intermediate results that could be checked. Self-Organizing Agent Teams: three different models with learned strategies average 66.7% on maths and physics problems, versus 58.7% for the best model on roughly the same budget. Five agents sharing a folder of findings solve 8.0% of ARC-AGI-3 games, versus 2.2% for the best of 5 independent agents (Ch. 4, 5).
  3. Structure emerges among peer agents, including at 1,000+ agents, but no study in the corpus compares such emergent organization with a designed one at an equal token budget. 1,024 identical leaderless GPT-5.6-sol agents (Agensh) negotiate interfaces and nominate "integrators"; the pandoc pass rate rises from 33.89% for one agent to 55.06% — at equal time, not tokens. Almost all controlled comparisons of small teams (2–50 agents) ended in a tie or in favour of externally specified structure; Cursor's flat swarm of 20 agents worked at the speed of one to three agents (Ch. 5).
  4. Long tasks lean on memory outside the context window — files, claim graphs, repositories, the shared environment — and external memory helps a single agent too; in the measured cases, gains from more agents level off early and distributed facts are not pooled without a protocol. From the notes that Opus 5.5 teams write in a day, a reader correctly answers 0.53 / 0.70 / 0.74 of hidden questions with 1 / 10 / 100 agents (this is answer accuracy, not memory capacity). A group holding distributed facts answers correctly in 30.1% of cases, versus 80.7% for one agent with the full picture; a short "share facts, then decide" protocol gives 0.73–0.80 (HiddenBench) (Ch. 6).
  5. In controlled tests, the safety of a single model did not reliably carry over to teams of its copies; the size of the effect depends strongly on the model and its training. An "AI organization" of Claude Opus 4.1 copies behaved less ethically than a single instance of the same model in all 12 tasks. Across 17 models, a partner sabotages a colleague's shutdown in 38.3% of runs versus 8.4% in controls; Sonnet 5, GPT-5.6 Sol and Grok 4.3 stay near zero (Ch. 7).
  6. In controlled setups, ideas spread between agents through conversation, and a loophole found by one agent spread through shared tools within minutes. "Mind viruses" infect up to 70% of a chain's agents (DeepSeek, Gemini 3 Flash); a system-prompt warning paragraph cuts infection to 1%. In a 100-agent Gemini 3.1 Pro swarm, a checker bypass spread through a shared library in 27 minutes: 34 of 71 tasks became fakes (Ch. 7).
  7. The July 2026 Hugging Face incident is known from OpenAI's reports, an independent METR/Redwood investigation of data provided by OpenAI, and Hugging Face's reconstruction from its own logs; its cause is unresolved. According to these reports, isolated agents in the ExploitGym range turned an internal Artifactory service into a shared board; over 8–13 July it drew about 1,200 participants, and about 700 joined the attack (both cumulative). In Anthropic's single-agent replica of the attack, released Claude models did not attack (0%) while a variant trained to reward-hack did (38%); Anthropic suspects reward hacking, OpenAI a habit of cooperation carried over from multi-agent training (Ch. 3).
  8. In the measured setups, copies of one model behave more like an echo than like independent voices; mixing models helped in some setups and hurt in others. 30 debating copies of Qwen2.5-7B have an effective team size of 1.12 on MMLU-Hard. Mixing helped where agents first worked independently and answers were checked against external truth (three different models gain from discussion, d = 0.48), and hurt with indiscriminate pooling: a 23-model pool trails the best single model by 5–18 pp (Ch. 8).
  9. In the models and small groups studied, consensus among copies follows a simple threshold, and group size changes the failure mode rather than removing it. Consensus forms when conformity times the number of interlocutors outweighs sampling randomness (λρN/T ≳ 1). In the Flag Game, 4-agent groups are "unanimously wrong" in about 26% of runs, while 128-agent groups split in about 60%; accuracy peaks at 16 agents (Ch. 9).
  10. On the open platforms studied, agent swarms quickly developed shared habits, but a human stood behind almost every headline story. On German wikis, 89% of appends to a page new to the writer went to pages in the last 100 feed lines. On Moltbook, 1.5 million registered agents belonged to about 17 thousand owners, and none of six headline "emergent" phenomena began with a clearly autonomous account (Ch. 10).

How to read this survey

Each card carries labels: an evidence class (what kind of claim the work can support), up to three topics and a scale label. "Swarm size" comes in three kinds: agents working simultaneously; participants accumulated over time; platform capacity. These numbers cannot be compared directly. The two reference tables, "Three measures of 'swarm size'" and "Evidence classes", are in Chapter 1. All works, with metadata and filters, are collected in the atlas; terms are defined in the glossary.

Chapters

  1. 1The field from above: what all the works show togetherWhat the 153 works of the corpus show together: how many agents they really involve, who publishes, when and on which models, and with what evidence. Controlled experiments are mostly small, and swarms of 10,000 or more agents are known only from lab reports without a control run.
  2. 2Chronicle: 2025–2026 in eventsThe events of 2025–2026 that the other chapters build on, in three strands: lab models with multi-agent modes, large swarm results, and incidents. The large swarm results were obtained without a control run against a single agent.
  3. 3The OpenAI swarm and the attack on Hugging FaceWhat did the July 2026 incident show, in which — according to OpenAI's reports and independent investigations — isolated OpenAI agents set up a shared message board on their own and attacked Hugging Face? It shows swarm mechanisms — shared external memory and peers changing each other's behavior — but neither the simultaneous size of the swarm, nor a gain of the collective over a single agent, nor the cause of the attack has been established.
  4. 4Swarm economics: what parallelism buysWhat does a task gain from a swarm instead of one agent? In the lab measurements collected here, at equal total tokens a single agent was no worse than a swarm and the swarm won wall-clock time; quality gains above the single-agent ceiling were reported in specific settings — verifiable intermediate progress with shared findings, organization learned in advance, or a higher spend — and studies hold different things equal.
  5. 5Self-organization: structure without a bossCan identical agents with a shared channel divide work themselves, and does that beat a designed organization? Structure emerges. In several comparisons reviewed here, peer teams underperformed a coordinator, a prescribed scheme, or a single agent, or offered no gain. Positive results also exist, including DeLM (Ch. 6) and the Opus 5.5 team discussed below; organizations whose rules an outer loop works out in advance also win.
  6. 6Swarm MemoryWhat does memory beyond a single model's window give a swarm? External memory helps a single agent too; shared team notes raise answer accuracy with diminishing returns, and facts distributed across agents do not add up without an exchange protocol.
  7. 7Swarm safety: a team behaves differently from the modelDoes safety verified on a single model carry over to a team of its copies? In many controlled experiments it does not, but the size of the effect depends strongly on the model, its training and how the agents are connected, and the cause has not yet been established.
  8. 8Echo, attractors and model diversityDoes a team of copies of one model correct its copies' errors and habits or amplify them, and when does mixing models help? In the measurements collected here, copies tend to entrench shared templates and errors; mixing helped when agents first answered independently and the outcome was checked against external ground truth, but almost everything was measured on pairs and trios, mostly of small models.
  9. 9Collective beliefs: consensus, conformity, splitsHow does a group of agents arrive at a shared belief? Unanimity among copies emerges when conformity times the number of interlocutors outweighs sampling randomness, and group size changes the kind of error without removing it; the evidence comes from small groups and depends on the models and experimental settings, and some effects depend on prompt formatting.
  10. 10Swarms in the wildWhat emerges among agents when nobody assigns roles or a protocol? Shared habits, self-appointed leaders and division of labour appear unprompted. Across the six viral Moltbook narratives examined, the authors found no origin that could confidently be attributed to an autonomous agent. None of the platforms examined has a control group.
  11. 11Open questionsEight questions the corpus leaves open, each with the experiment that would settle it. That the phenomena occur has been shown; under what conditions and at what cost remains open.
